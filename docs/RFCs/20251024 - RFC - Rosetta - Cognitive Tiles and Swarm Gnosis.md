## **Motivation** {#motivation}

Modern knowledge systems face challenges in **portability**, **provenance**, and **efficient retrieval**. Research outputs and AI inferences are often locked in monolithic formats or black-box models, making it hard to audit how information was produced or to reuse it in new contexts. **Cognitive Tiles** address this by breaking knowledge into self-contained, content-addressed “tiles” that carry their own context, proofs, and lineage. The goal is a **“Swarm Gnosis”** substrate: a decentralized swarm of agents that can discover, verify, and evolve knowledge tiles collaboratively. This approach draws inspiration from content-addressable networks (like IPFS) and reproducible research practices, aiming to make meaning **portable, auditable, and economically decodable** across agents. By attaching provenance metadata and **proof-carrying data**, each tile enables trust and reuse – helping to build credibility and reproducibility into the fabric of knowledge[ardc.edu.au](https://ardc.edu.au/article/data-provenance-metadata-builds-trust-credibility-and-reproducibility/#:~:text=Data%20provenance%20metadata%20enables%20interpretation,Here%20are%20some%20typical). In short, this framework is motivated by the need for a distributed knowledge base where **every piece of content knows what it is, where it came from, and what it’s for**, allowing agents to reason and trade off which information to retrieve or compute based on cost and value.

## **Tile Schema and Canonicalization** {#tile-schema-and-canonicalization}

**Cognitive Tiles** are designed as self-describing Merkle-DAG objects. Each tile’s content is serialized in a *canonical form* to ensure consistent hashing and content addressing. We adopt a canonical JSON encoding (following RFC 8785\) so that semantically identical data yields the same hash[datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8785#:~:text=Cryptographic%20operations%20like%20hashing%20and,%C2%B6)[datatracker.ietf.org](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8785#:~:text=This%20document%20describes%20the%20JSON,%C2%B6). This involves deterministic key ordering, normalized number formats, and stripping of insignificant whitespace[connect2id.com](https://connect2id.com/blog/how-to-secure-json-objects-with-hmac#:~:text=The%20JSON%20Canonicalization%20Scheme%20,applying%20digital%20signatures%20and%20HMACs)[connect2id.com](https://connect2id.com/blog/how-to-secure-json-objects-with-hmac#:~:text=When%20JCS%20is%20applied%20to,sample%20JSON%20object%20we%20get) – similar to the JSON Canonicalization Scheme which was created to make cryptographic hashing and signing of JSON data reliable[connect2id.com](https://connect2id.com/blog/how-to-secure-json-objects-with-hmac#:~:text=The%20JSON%20Canonicalization%20Scheme%20,applying%20digital%20signatures%20and%20HMACs). Using a stable canonical form, each tile is content-addressed by a cryptographic hash of its bytes (e.g. SHA-256). We employ **self-describing CIDs (Content IDs)** as in IPLD/IPFS: the identifier encodes not just the hash digest but also the hash function and data codec[ipld.io](https://ipld.io/docs/motivation/data-to-data-structures/#:~:text=For%20links%20we%20use%20a,just%20a%20series%20of%20bytes). This multihash/multicodec scheme provides *hash agility* – e.g. a tile can be upgraded to a stronger hash algorithm in the future while still being distinguishable, since the CID prefix denotes which algorithm produced it[ipld.io](https://ipld.io/docs/motivation/data-to-data-structures/#:~:text=For%20one%20thing%2C%20there%20are,hash%20of%20this%20particular%20algorithm). In contrast to bespoke, fixed hashing schemes, using community-standard multihashes ensures future-proofing; new hash functions can be introduced without breaking existing links[ipld.io](https://ipld.io/docs/motivation/data-to-data-structures/#:~:text=For%20one%20thing%2C%20there%20are,hash%20of%20this%20particular%20algorithm).

Each tile contains a **header** (with metadata like type, creation timestamp, author ID, license, etc.) and a **payload** which may include: (a) the core content or a latent “slug” representing it, (b) links (CIDs) to any parent or source tiles (enabling version lineage), (c) proof or witness data (e.g. computations or test results supporting the content), and (d) digital signatures or attestations. The tile’s hash (CID) serves as a globally unique, content-derived address. This makes tiles **immutable** – any change to content or metadata produces a new CID, forming a Merkle-DAG. By linking tiles to their predecessors, we get an **append-only version history**: an update to a knowledge tile results in a new tile that carries a pointer to the previous version’s CID. Tiles thereby form a directed acyclic graph of versions where *each modification is a new node linked to its parent, forming an immutable chain of evolution*[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=match%20at%20L215%20Each%20modification,immutable%20chain%20of%20creative%20evolution). This Merkle-DAG lineage approach, much like Git commits or IPFS objects, allows reproducible traversal of knowledge history and natural deduplication (identical sub-structures can point to the same underlying tile). It also enables verification of integrity: given a tile’s CID, any tampering with its content or links will yield a different hash and thus break the chain.

**Trade-offs:** We choose a multihash content addressing scheme over a single fixed-hash approach to ensure flexibility (e.g. to migrate from SHA-256 to SHA-512 or quantum-resistant hashes later). The cost is slightly larger identifiers and the need for all participants to support multiple algorithms, but the benefit is long-term security and interoperability[ipld.io](https://ipld.io/docs/motivation/data-to-data-structures/#:~:text=For%20one%20thing%2C%20there%20are,hash%20of%20this%20particular%20algorithm). The tile’s JSON schema is designed to minimize unnecessary variance (using ordered keys, etc.) to further ensure hash stability. Alternate encodings like CBOR could be used for size/binary efficiency, but JSON with canonicalization is human-readable and has standardized normalization[connect2id.com](https://connect2id.com/blog/how-to-secure-json-objects-with-hmac#:~:text=The%20JSON%20Canonicalization%20Scheme%20,applying%20digital%20signatures%20and%20HMACs). For each tile we compute a CID that includes a *codec identifier* (e.g. indicating JSON) and the hash digest, so any agent encountering a CID can immediately know how to interpret the bytes[ipld.io](https://ipld.io/docs/motivation/data-to-data-structures/#:~:text=For%20links%20we%20use%20a,just%20a%20series%20of%20bytes). Overall, these canonicalization and addressing practices make tile content **content-addressable and verifiable** in a persistent way: anyone can independently hash a tile’s content to confirm it matches the claimed CID, which is fundamental for trustless verification and deduplication across the swarm.

## **Swarm Design: Discovery, Routing and Replication** {#swarm-design:-discovery,-routing-and-replication}

The **Swarm Gnosis** network is a decentralized peer-to-peer network where nodes (agents) cooperate to store and retrieve tiles. We leverage a hybrid of **Kademlia DHT** and gossip protocols for discovery and routing. In this design, each tile’s CID serves as its lookup key in the DHT. Peers in the swarm run a Kademlia-based routing service (such as libp2p’s Kademlia DHT implementation) to publish and find provider records for CIDs[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/discovery-routing/overview/#:~:text=,closest%20to%20the%20content%E2%80%99s%20identifier). In essence, when a node stores a tile, it announces to the DHT that it can provide that CID; any node needing that tile can perform a DHT lookup to find peer addresses close to that key[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/discovery-routing/overview/#:~:text=,closest%20to%20the%20content%E2%80%99s%20identifier). Kademlia’s XOR metric ensures lookups take $O(\\log N)$ hops and finds at least one peer storing the content (assuming it’s available). This content routing via DHT decouples discovery from physical location: ask the network for a hash, get back one or more peers who have it, then fetch via direct connections (e.g. Bitswap or HTTP).

In addition to exact CID lookups, we introduce **semantic indexes** in the DHT for more flexible discovery. For example, for a given tag or keyword (e.g. a “glyph tag” that labels a tile’s topic), a subset of peers act as indexers by storing mappings from tag → list of CIDs of tiles with that tag. Similarly, we can index parent→children relations: upon creating a new version of a tile, a node could store a DHT entry under a key like “supersedes:\<oldCID\>” pointing to the new CID. This allows queries like “find tiles derived from X” without knowing their hashes in advance. Such index keys could be hashed themselves (to distribute across the DHT) and point to small lists of relevant CIDs. While DHTs excel at exact-match lookups, range or full-text queries are more challenging. We mitigate this by using controlled vocabularies (e.g. canonical tags or glyphs) that can be hashed as keys, and by supplementing with gossip-based search. **GossipPub** (the libp2p GossipSub protocol) is used for propagating announcements of new tiles or hot topics. Peers subscribe to relevant topics (like a tag or a community of interest); when a new tile is created with that tag, a short announcement is gossiped, carrying the tile’s CID and a brief descriptor. GossipSub ensures reliable, fan-out dissemination without flooding[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=libp2p%20currently%20uses%20a%20design,maintain%20a%20message%20delivery%20network) – peers gossip about message IDs and only fetch unseen items[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=libp2p%20currently%20uses%20a%20design,maintain%20a%20message%20delivery%20network). This hybrid means: for known keys (CIDs, tags, specific authors), the DHT provides structured discovery; for emergent or trending info, gossip provides timely awareness.

Every node maintains a **cache and pin set**. By default, when a node retrieves a tile (e.g. to answer a query or use in reasoning), it **caches** it locally and advertises itself as a provider for that CID (for some time). This opportunistic replication means popular tiles naturally propagate to more peers, improving availability. However, since storage is finite, nodes periodically run *garbage collection* to discard unpinned, least-recently-used content[docs.ipfs.tech](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/persistence/#:~:text=Nodes%20on%20the%20IPFS%20network,process%20is%20called%20garbage%20collection). To ensure important knowledge isn’t lost, the system uses **pinning** for persistence. If a node deems a tile valuable long-term (or is incentivized to host it), it pins that tile – meaning it will retain that data and not garbage-collect it[docs.ipfs.tech](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/persistence/#:~:text=Nodes%20on%20the%20IPFS%20network,process%20is%20called%20garbage%20collection)[docs.ipfs.tech](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/persistence/#:~:text=To%20ensure%20that%20data%20persists,to%20keep%20on%20IPFS%20indefinitely). Pinning essentially marks content as permanent on that node, similar to how IPFS nodes “pin” content they must keep. Organizations or communities can run dedicated pinning nodes (or use IPFS-cluster style coordination) to ensure certain datasets/tiles are always available. Our swarm also implements an **anti-entropy protocol** to continuously reconcile data between peers. On a schedule or on demand, peers exchange hash sketches (Merkle trees or bloom filters) of the tiles they have, to identify any missing tiles that should be replicated. This is analogous to anti-entropy repairs in distributed databases where nodes compare hashes to sync only the differing parts[geeksforgeeks.org](https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/system-design/anti-entropy-in-distributed-systems/#:~:text=,The%20data%20exchange%20is%20designed). For example, a peer might detect via hashes that it’s missing some tiles that its close DHT neighbors have advertised; it can then pull those in the background, ensuring redundancy. Since knowledge tiles are generally small (text or encoded data, not huge media files), this lightweight anti-entropy and **gossip-based replication** is feasible without overwhelming bandwidth. It provides **eventual consistency** – over time, all nodes that subscribe to a certain knowledge domain converge to have the relevant tiles[geeksforgeeks.org](https://www.geeksforgeeks.org/system-design/anti-entropy-in-distributed-systems/#:~:text=,all%20nodes%20converge%20to%20the) (assuming they have resources and permission).

To optimize routing, the swarm may also use **clustered peering and supernodes**. Peers can form interest-based clusters (e.g. a cluster for a specific ontology or project) such that intra-cluster tile requests can be served quickly (a bit like a locality-aware DHT). For wider network queries, we rely on the Kademlia backbone. We also allow **HTTP peering** as a fallback: some nodes may expose HTTP APIs (RESTful endpoints for `tile.get(CID)` etc.) or act as gateways. This is useful for lightweight clients or integration with web apps – an agent can use HTTP to fetch a tile from a known peer URL if DHT is not accessible (similar to how IPFS has public gateways). The design thus embraces libp2p P2P protocols primarily, but doesn’t exclude federated or client-server usage when convenient.

**Query semantics:** Agents can query not just by exact CID, but by various metadata: search by tile type, by keywords, by creator, or by trust score. These queries are resolved by either hitting special index tiles (e.g. a tile that serves as an index of “all tiles about topic X”) or by distributed querying. For example, to find tiles with a given keyword, an agent might hash the keyword and perform a DHT lookup for that key, getting back a set of CIDs (as stored by some indexer node). Trust scores or reputations might be queried similarly (e.g. a mapping of public key → score, stored in DHT). We anticipate layering a **content discovery service** on top of the basic DHT to handle richer queries (possibly a variation of the Graph Index like The Graph for indexing tile metadata off-chain, if necessary[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=Query%20performance%20represents%20a%20critical,aligns%20with%20PromptChain%E2%80%99s%20architectural%20principles)). However, a pure peer-to-peer approach is preferred for openness: that could mean using *flood queries* within a scope (ask peers in your cluster and have them ask others, bounded by TTL) for complex queries. Caching is critical – query results (lists of CIDs) will themselves be cached or even materialized as tiles (query result tiles) to avoid repeated wide-area searches.

Finally, **caching and replication policies** are tuned to knowledge-specific usage. Unlike large media, knowledge tiles are small and often referenced by other tiles. Therefore, nodes aggressively pre-fetch and cache tiles that are linked from content they are consuming. (E.g. if an agent pulls a tile that has parent links, it may pre-fetch those parents to understand context.) This *transitive caching* ensures that if an agent has a tile, it likely has its ancestry or related pieces, improving locality for verification. We also integrate **pinning services**: nodes can outsource persistence to pinning servers or cloud storage, which is useful for edge devices with limited storage. Pinning services register on the swarm and advertise “I will store any tile for X fee or as per policy.” When a critical mass of independent nodes pins a tile, it achieves permanence (similar to multiple archival nodes). In summary, the swarm design uses *DHT for scalable routing*, *gossip for timely distribution*, and *caching/pinning for resilience*. Knowledge flows through the network quickly for popular items, and remains available long-term through replication on willing nodes.

## **Versioning and Knowledge Evolution** {#versioning-and-knowledge-evolution}

Knowledge is not static, so the system must support **versioning, branching, and merging** of tiles. Each tile is immutable once created, but can be *superseded* by new tiles. We implement an **append-only version graph**: when a tile’s content is revised, a new tile is minted with a pointer (`prev` or `parent` field) to the original. This yields a lineage chain (or tree, if multiple branches) of versions. For example, a hypothesis tile might be updated as new evidence arrives – the new version tile will cite the prior version’s CID as its parent. This approach is similar to how Git tracks versions via commit DAGs and how IPFS can model evolving files by linking to new versions[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=while%20the%20Merkle%20DAG%20structure,child%20CID%20relationships)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=Our%20version%20control%20system%2C%20built,history%20that%20informs%20both%20attribution). It means any node can trace backward from the latest tile to its origin through the chain of CIDs, enabling **provenance and change auditing**.

We compare two approaches: **CRDTs vs explicit version DAGs**. CRDTs (Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types) allow concurrent edits to merge automatically by mathematical rules, guaranteeing eventual consistency without conflicts[pages.lip6.fr](https://pages.lip6.fr/Marc.Shapiro/papers/RR-7687.pdf#:~:text=%5BPDF%5D%20Conflict,to%20a%20correct%20common%20state). This is powerful for collaborative real-time edits on *primitive data structures* (counters, sets, JSON documents etc.). However, for *semantic knowledge units*, blind merges could produce incoherent or untrusted results. We opt for a more Git-like model where concurrent branches can exist and a deliberate merge operation (which produces a new tile) is used to reconcile them. In other words, if two agents independently update a tile (say two scientists independently add data to a tile representing a dataset), we will have two child tiles branching from the original. A merge would be a new tile that cites both those versions as parents (creating a **merge tile** that supersedes them). This retains human oversight or at least algorithmic diff+merge logic at a higher semantic level, rather than always auto-merging at byte-level. That said, for certain structured content (like sets of facts or contributions), we could employ CRDT-type tiles (e.g. a tile whose payload is a GSet of elements) which would merge automatically if two variants appear. The framework is flexible: it supports CRDT tiles (with appropriate *merge functions*) for cases where deterministic merges are desired, and **version graph** for other cases where explicit merges or forks are better.

To coordinate versions, we introduce special **supersedence markers**. A **deprecation tile** is a small tile that simply marks another tile as deprecated (perhaps with a reason). Similarly, an **index tile** can serve as a “latest version pointer” – e.g. for a frequently updated tile, one can have a stable CID (index tile) that always lists the current version CID (and maybe N previous versions). This index tile can itself be content-addressed or referenced by a human-friendly name (like a DOI or IPNS-style mutable pointer). When a new version comes out, the index tile is updated (which means its CID changes, but clients who know the old index tile can find the new via a known naming service or DHT mapping of a stable name to latest CID). This approach decouples human references from the content hashes, similar to how IPNS (the InterPlanetary Naming System) provides a mutable name on top of IPFS CIDs[medium.com](https://medium.com/mvp-workshop/a-closer-look-to-the-inter-planetary-file-system-b3f3af31a3c7#:~:text=two%20main%20reasons%20why%20versioning,essential%2C%20first%20is%20being). In our design, *every tile can carry a “supersedes” field listing which prior tile(s) it replaces*. Agents who come across an older tile can follow these links to find newer knowledge (and conversely, new tiles link back so one can trace history).

We emphasize **human-readable change representation**. When a tile is created as an update of another, the creator is encouraged to include a brief *change summary* in the metadata (analogous to a Git commit message or Wikipedia edit summary). Additionally, because the content is structured (or at least we can compare JSON), a tile may include a *reverse delta* – a machine-computable diff from its parent. For text content, this could be a simple diff hunk; for structured data, a list of added/removed facts. This makes it easier for an agent (or person) to quickly see *what changed*. Tiles also support **reverse diffs**: given tile A and B, if A cites B as parent, B could include enough info to reconstruct itself from A plus a patch, if needed (though storing reverse patches in each tile is optional and increases size). The principle is to facilitate *efficient version transitions* and **summarize drift** over time.

**Branching and merging policies:** The swarm does not enforce a single canonical version for each knowledge item – forks can coexist (just as scientific hypotheses can diverge). However, communities of agents may agree on conventions to merge results for a unified view. For instance, if two tiles represent the same underlying concept (identified by say a stable URI) but with different content, a reconciliation tile could unify them or mark one as preferred. Agents could choose which branch to follow based on trust (e.g. they trust a particular curator’s version). This resembles how Wikipedia handles content disputes by discussion and eventual merging, or how multiple forks of an open-source project might eventually merge. The system provides *metadata fields* to help: a tile can declare if it **obsoletes** some earlier tile (stronger than supersedes, implying the old one should no longer be used), or if it’s a **minor revision** vs major (for optional backward compatibility semantics).

Deprecation is handled by consensus or authority: e.g. a tile creator or a quorum of witnesses can issue a deprecation tile that essentially says “Tile X is invalid or retracted”. Since tiles are immutable, we cannot delete X from the network, but agents can subscribe to trust feeds that inform them “don’t use X, see Y instead.” The swarm’s role is to propagate these notices (perhaps via a DHT key like `deprecate:<X>` mapping to deprecation tile ID). Splitting and merging of tiles are higher-level editorial decisions: splitting might occur if one tile grows too broad and needs to be broken into two more focused tiles. In practice this would produce two new tiles and likely deprecate the original, with clear links among them. Merging tiles (two independent tiles found to be duplicates or overlapping) might produce a new tile that has both as parents and amalgamates their information.

In summary, **knowledge evolution is tracked as a graph** of immutable states (tiles) with links. This yields a transparent audit trail: one can traverse back to the origin or see alternative branches. The design follows modern version control principles (append-only, auditable history) rather than destructive updates. This also aids reproducibility: earlier versions remain accessible (with their CIDs), so results citing an older tile can still fetch it for reference or verification. The combination of machine-readable diffs and human-readable summaries ensures that both agents and people can interpret changes. As research suggests, unifying CRDT-style convergence with version control’s history is an active area[martin.kleppmann.com](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2022/03/28/rainbowfs-workshop.html#:~:text=But%20we%20already%20have%20an,and%20the%20world%20of%20CRDTs) – our approach leans toward explicit version tracking with optional CRDT merging where appropriate, to balance consistency and accountability.

## **Trust, Provenance, and Verification (Proof-Carrying Data)** {#trust,-provenance,-and-verification-(proof-carrying-data)}

Trust in the knowledge swarm is achieved through **proof-carrying data and verifiable provenance**. Each cognitive tile can carry **cryptographic proofs or signatures** that attest to its validity, origin, or the process by which it was created. This concept is inspired by Proof-Carrying Data (PCD) in distributed systems, where every message (or data item) is augmented with a proof that certain properties hold[ic-people.epfl.ch](https://ic-people.epfl.ch/~achiesa/docs/CT10.pdf#:~:text=We%20propose%20a%20new%20approach%2C,comply%20with%20the%20specified%20properties)[ic-people.epfl.ch](https://ic-people.epfl.ch/~achiesa/docs/CT10.pdf#:~:text=Our%20approach%2C%20proof,final%20output%20attests%20that%20the). In our context, a tile might include a proof that the data was derived following a particular method or that it satisfies certain constraints. For example, a tile containing the result of an experiment could carry a proof that “these results come from executing code X on dataset Y” – effectively a **witness** that can be independently verified.

Tiles are signed by their creators or curators using digital signatures (we use Ed25519 by default for its strong security and speed[blog.ipfs.tech](https://blog.ipfs.tech/2025-08-ed25519/#:~:text=,bytes%20versus%20256%2B%20for%20RSA), but secp256k1 or others can be used as well). A signature on a tile attests that *a particular identity approves or vouches for the content*. Because Ed25519 public keys are only 32 bytes and signatures 64 bytes, it’s feasible to sign every tile. These **content signatures** give authenticity: any agent can verify that the tile indeed came from the holder of a given private key[blog.ipfs.tech](https://blog.ipfs.tech/2025-08-ed25519/#:~:text=Ed25519%20is%20a%20type%20of,quickly%20establishing%20trust%20among%20peers). In distributed networks like this swarm, such signatures are essential for establishing trust quickly[blog.ipfs.tech](https://blog.ipfs.tech/2025-08-ed25519/#:~:text=Ed25519%20is%20a%20type%20of,quickly%20establishing%20trust%20among%20peers). We can build a web-of-trust or reputation system around these identities: e.g. some public keys might correspond to known institutions or experts, and their signed tiles carry more weight; unknown keys might need their data to be corroborated or undergo more scrutiny.

Beyond basic signatures, the framework supports **attestations and receipts**. An *attestation* is like a certificate or statement, possibly issued by a third party, saying “I attest to the following about tile Z (hash …): ...”. For instance, a peer review board could issue an attestation that a tile (paper or result) has been peer reviewed and found sound. These attestations can be bundled with the tile or discoverable via the tile’s CID (e.g. a DHT lookup on `attest:<CID>` returns a list of signatures or references to attestation tiles). A **receipt-style trace** is a record that an agent performed some operation on the tile (like verified it, or used it in a computation) at a certain time. Whenever an agent *decodes or verifies* a tile, it can produce a signed receipt of that event. For example: *Agent A verified tile X against its source data on 2025-10-24T12:00Z*. These receipts can be collected to build confidence that tiles have been vetted. They also enable reproducibility: one can follow the chain of receipts and re-run the steps (potentially using the same container or script provided in the witness data).

Crucially, we embed **provenance metadata** in each tile. This includes fields like `origin` (e.g. references to source datasets or prior tiles), `method` (e.g. an identifier for the protocol or code used), and `environment` (e.g. software versions, parameters). This is analogous to recording an experiment’s methodology so others can reproduce it. The importance of capturing such provenance is well-known for trust and reproducibility[ardc.edu.au](https://ardc.edu.au/article/data-provenance-metadata-builds-trust-credibility-and-reproducibility/#:~:text=Data%20provenance%20metadata%20enables%20interpretation,Here%20are%20some%20typical) – it allows others to interpret and validate the results. In practice, a tile that presents a conclusion will often link to one or more *witness packages*: these could be other tiles containing raw data, Jupyter notebooks, scripts, or even ZK-SNARK verifying keys that substantiate the conclusion. For instance, a “reproducible research playbook” tile might include a reference to a Docker image hash or a notebook (perhaps stored via IPFS) that can regenerate the results. This aligns with initiatives in scientific computing to package and share the entire workflow (data \+ code \+ environment)[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6516719/#:~:text=Embedding%20data%20provenance%20into%20the,data%2C%20thereby%20facilitating%20reproducible%20research).

We treat **witness code as potentially untrusted**, thus any executable included (like a script to recompute a result) is executed in a sandboxed manner for safety. Techniques like WebAssembly sandboxing are leveraged to run untrusted code with no access to the host environment unless explicitly granted[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/61709122/can-i-use-webassembly-to-safely-execute-untrusted-user-code-within-my-web-app#:~:text=8). For example, if a tile includes a snippet of Python to verify a theorem proof, an agent can run that in a WASM or container sandbox that only allows CPU and memory, but no network or file system (unless needed for the computation). This ensures that incorporating executable witnesses does not expose agents to malware. The sandbox provides a controlled API – often just a way to output results or a boolean “verification passed” flag. As Stack Overflow users note, WebAssembly’s design means code can’t do I/O or harm unless the host explicitly permits it[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/61709122/can-i-use-webassembly-to-safely-execute-untrusted-user-code-within-my-web-app#:~:text=8), making it suitable for *proof-carrying code execution* in our use case.

**When to verify vs attest vs quarantine:** Not all tiles will be fully verified by every agent due to resource limits. We establish a policy akin to risk management: if a tile comes from a highly trusted source (e.g. signed by a reputable authority) and carries attestations, an agent might *accept it on trust* (at least for non-critical use) without re-running all proofs. This is analogous to how a browser trusts a certificate signed by a known CA. However, if the content is critical (e.g. a medical decision) or from an unknown source, the agent should **verify** it end-to-end – meaning execute the witness or check the cryptographic proof provided. Tiles can declare a **verification level** required (for example, a tile might say “requires data verification on first use”). Agents maintain a **quarantine** list for tiles that are suspicious or fail checks. If a tile’s signature doesn’t verify or its proof fails, the agent marks it as untrusted (and could propagate a warning to others via an attestation: “tile X failed verification”). Optionally, the swarm could support **reputation scores** for data: tiles (and their authors) accumulate a trust score based on others’ feedback, which agents use to decide verification urgency. In practice, an efficient use of resources would be: **attest first, verify on demand**. That is, rely on prior attestations/receipts if available (e.g. “5 other nodes verified this tile, and it’s signed by someone I trust”), but be prepared to do your own verification if those are lacking or if the stakes are high. This approach balances assurance and performance.

We also incorporate emerging tech like **zero-knowledge proofs (ZK)** for privacy-preserving verification. A tile can carry a ZK-proof that a certain property holds without revealing all details. For example, a tile might state a conclusion that depends on some confidential data; it could include a ZK proof that “I applied algorithm A to dataset committed in hash H and got result R” without revealing the dataset itself. Such *ZK attestations* allow participants to trust results computed on sensitive data because the proof verifies correctness. Our design allows these – the tile would include the proof and the necessary verification key or circuit ID. Agents can run a ZK verifier (which is fast for many modern ZK-SNARKs) to confirm the claim. This is optional and used when needed (e.g. proprietary data or when minimizing data transfer).

All provenance and verification info is linked via content addressing, which makes it **audit-able**. One can start at a result tile, traverse back through its `origin` links to raw data tiles, check each transformation either manually, via provided code, or via included proofs. Because everything is identified by hashes, the chain of evidence cannot be faked without breaking a hash link. This creates a high level of trust in well-formed tiles – essentially *proof-carrying knowledge*. Participants in the swarm can thus focus their attention on reviewing the most critical or contentious pieces (since trivial verifications can be automated and notarized), scaling human oversight.

## **Retrieval and Agent Policies for Economic Decoding** {#retrieval-and-agent-policies-for-economic-decoding}

Agents in the swarm face the challenge of **selective attention**: there may be thousands of tiles potentially relevant to a query or task, but decoding or examining each in detail has a computational or economic cost. To address this, we incorporate principles from *active inference* and *rational metareasoning* to guide retrieval. Each agent maintains an internal model of its information needs and uncertainties. When a query arrives (e.g. a user question or an AI reasoning task), the agent estimates the **expected free energy (EFE)** or value-of-information for candidate tiles before fully decoding them. Expected free energy is a measure from active inference that quantifies the expected reduction in uncertainty (and gain in utility) from an action, minus its cost[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10425v1#:~:text=dynamic%20interplay%20allows%20the%20agent,costs%20associated%20with%20belief%20updating). In this context, an action is “decode this tile” or “fetch this tile’s content.” The agent will prefer to first fetch or open tiles that are predicted to *reduce its uncertainty about the query the most* per unit cost[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10425v1#:~:text=dynamic%20interplay%20allows%20the%20agent,costs%20associated%20with%20belief%20updating). For example, if two tiles might answer a question but one is much more likely (or much smaller to process), the agent focuses on that one.

This approach implements a form of **expected utility retrieval**: each tile has an associated *belief about its relevance* (from metadata or prior uses) and a *cost* (e.g. in CPU time, network latency, token count if feeding to an LLM, etc.). The agent computes a score like $score \= expected\_gain / cost$, and picks the highest scores first. This way, trivial or irrelevant tiles are pruned out early, and only potentially informative ones are expanded. It’s analogous to **progressive search** or multi-step retrieval: get a pointer or summary first, then decide to get details. In fact, tiles support this inherently: because some tiles may just contain a latent slug or a summary plus a link to full data, an agent can decide to not hydrate the slug unless needed.

Economically, we model that each agent has a **budget** (could be literal currency, or time, or just a cognitive budget in AI systems). **Decoding costs** might include using API calls (e.g. if an agent is an LLM that must ingest content into context window, that has a token cost), CPU cycles to run a verification, or network bandwidth to fetch data. We instrument agents to log and possibly *pay for* these actions (in a research simulation, payment could be abstract; in a deployed system, one might imagine microtransactions for using others’ data or compute). Over time, the agent learns from feedback which types of tiles or sources tend to *“pay for themselves”* – i.e. yield valuable info – and which are often dead ends. This is achieved via a **telemetry and reinforcement loop**: after each task, the agent can observe which tiles contributed to a successful answer or outcome. Those tiles or similar ones get a higher prior score next time (the agent’s policy is updated to favor them), whereas tiles that were fetched but turned out irrelevant get a lower expectation next time. This is akin to a multi-armed bandit optimization or learning-to-retrieve model that adjusts retrieval strategy based on past success.

Concretely, if an agent is frequently solving queries in a domain, it might notice that tiles signed by certain experts or coming from a certain “journal” tile have high value, so it will more readily fetch those. Conversely, a tile type that historically adds little new info might be skipped unless nothing else is available. This **experience-driven optimization** ensures that as the swarm operates, it becomes more efficient in routing attention to the right knowledge.

We also design the retrieval pipeline to integrate with advanced **Graph-RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation)** approaches. Traditional RAG for LLMs fetches raw text passages to feed into the model. Instead, our approach can return *tile URIs (CIDs) and concise descriptors* (like titles or summaries) rather than full passages on the first pass. This is inspired by GraphRAG methodologies that combine knowledge graphs with text retrieval[medium.com](https://medium.com/@zilliz_learn/graphrag-explained-enhancing-rag-with-knowledge-graphs-3312065f99e1#:~:text=To%20address%20such%20challenges%2C%20Microsoft,with%20the%20Milvus%20vector%20database). For example, suppose an LLM agent is asked a complex multi-hop question. Rather than immediately pulling in long paragraphs, it might query the swarm and get back a set of relevant tile identifiers plus short context: essentially a subgraph of related tile nodes. The agent can present or internally consider these references (“Tile 1234: ‘Discovery of Planet X’ by Dr. A; Tile 5678: ‘Orbital data’ by Agency B”). Then the agent can decide which of these to **drill down** into by fetching the full content. This two-stage process reduces cognitive load and context window size. It also makes the *retrieval step explainable*: the model knows the source *identifiers* and some metadata, so it can, for instance, cite them in an answer (“According to Dr. A’s report (tile 1234)…”) without even loading the entire report unless needed.

The knowledge graph aspect (hence “Graph” RAG) means we use relationships to guide retrieval. If a query involves connecting two concepts, a GraphRAG approach would find a path in the knowledge graph (sequence of related tiles)[medium.com](https://medium.com/@zilliz_learn/graphrag-explained-enhancing-rag-with-knowledge-graphs-3312065f99e1#:~:text=explain%20how%20GraphRAG%20works%20under,with%20the%20Milvus%20vector%20database). Our swarm’s data model – where tiles link to other tiles (citations, derivations) – naturally forms a **knowledge graph**. We can run algorithms on this graph: e.g. find shortest paths, perform subgraph matching for a question’s key entities, etc. Microsoft’s recent GraphRAG work formalizes how indexing and querying can happen on community clusters of a knowledge graph[medium.com](https://medium.com/@zilliz_learn/graphrag-explained-enhancing-rag-with-knowledge-graphs-3312065f99e1#:~:text=GraphRAG%2C%20a%20brand,with%20the%20Milvus%20vector%20database)[medium.com](https://medium.com/@zilliz_learn/graphrag-explained-enhancing-rag-with-knowledge-graphs-3312065f99e1#:~:text=Unlike%20a%20baseline%20RAG%20that,data%20based%20on%20their%20relationships). We envisage that specialized indexing nodes (or just powerful agents) might maintain graph indexes to quickly retrieve a subgraph given a query (using entity extraction and link traversal as in that approach). The result is a set of relevant tile IDs and perhaps a synthesized summary from the graph. The agent then uses those as context.

By returning only small headers (like each tile’s one-sentence description and trust score) initially, we achieve several things: **(1)** Lower latency and bandwidth: it’s faster to ship a 200-byte summary than a 10KB document. **(2)** Filtering: the agent might realize from the summary that some tiles aren’t needed (“Oh, that tile is about a different topic with similar name”). **(3)** Modular reasoning: an AI agent can treat each tile as a node it can query individually, rather than lumping all text together. For instance, it might first query a high-level tile for an overview, then decide to fetch a specific data tile for details.

The **agent policy** is thus to operate in layers: broad retrieval of *pointers* (with minimal info), analysis/planning on those, and targeted retrieval of actual content or proofs. This aligns with human research strategies (get references first, then read specific papers). Economically, it saves costs because the agent only “pays” (in time or tokens) for detailed content when it estimates it truly needs it. In essence, we implement **“free energy gating”** – only expend energy (compute) on a tile if it reduces expected free energy (uncertainty) significantly[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10425v1#:~:text=dynamic%20interplay%20allows%20the%20agent,costs%20associated%20with%20belief%20updating).

Additionally, we impose some *expected utility thresholds*: if the agent’s uncertainty is already low (like it’s pretty sure of an answer), it might stop and not retrieve more tiles even if available, unless required for confidence. Conversely, if uncertainty remains high, the agent can expand search to more obscure or costly tiles (like those stored on slow nodes or requiring heavy computation). This creates an anytime algorithm where the agent can trade off time vs accuracy gracefully.

Finally, we integrate an **economic cost model** for multi-agent settings: If certain tiles are served by certain peers at a price (imagine a peer charging micro-credits for using its compute-heavy verification service), the agent factors that into the utility. For example, verifying a result via a remote heavy computation might carry a micropayment; the agent will do so only if the added certainty is worth it. Conversely, an agent may “spend” some compute budget to verify a critical tile and then share that verification (receipt) with others, so they don’t all have to repeat it – creating an economy of knowledge verification.

To summarize, retrieval in this system is not a blind bulk fetch, but a **context-aware, selective process** guided by principles of expected information gain. Agents use tile metadata and relationships to decide *what to read vs. what to skip*, balancing **exploration and exploitation** of knowledge sources in a rational way. Over time, with feedback, the swarm becomes increasingly adept at answering questions with minimum resource use, focusing on the most *informative* tiles.

## **Pasigraphy Surface: Controlled Language for Meaning** {#pasigraphy-surface:-controlled-language-for-meaning}

To maximize interoperability and auditability of knowledge, we introduce an **EGC (Epistemic Graph Code)** – essentially a controlled representational language that tiles can use to express content in a **canonical, machine-readable form**. The vision is a *pasigraphy*: a universal writing system for knowledge that is unambiguous and compact. We draw on research in **controlled natural languages and semantic representations** such as AMR, UCCA, OWL, SBVR, and ACE, as well as the ambition of constructed languages like Ithkuil.

**Abstract Meaning Representation (AMR)** provides a way to represent the meaning of sentences as graphs of concepts and relations, abstracting away from word order and syntax[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_Meaning_Representation#:~:text=Abstract%20Meaning%20Representation%20%28AMR%29,as%20an%20international%20auxiliary%20language). For instance, different phrasings that mean the same thing map to the same AMR graph. This is attractive for our purposes: a tile’s knowledge could be encoded as an AMR-like graph (triples of relations) which is *language-neutral*. We want an EGC notation that can round-trip with such graphs so that if two tiles contain logically equivalent assertions, their EGC representation is identical (or at least can be normalized to identical). AMR itself is a bit biased to English[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_Meaning_Representation#:~:text=%28DAGs%29%2C%20comprising%20whole%20sentences,as%20an%20international%20auxiliary%20language) and is oriented toward sentence-level semantics, but its idea of a rooted directed acyclic graph of concepts is a good start. UCCA (Universal Conceptual Cognitive Annotation) similarly provides a cross-linguistic semantic framework, focusing on universal roles in semantics.

For formal knowledge, we also consider **OWL (Web Ontology Language)** and RDF, which represent knowledge as triples (subject-predicate-object). OWL has a strict logical basis (description logic), enabling reasoning, but it’s verbose and not meant to be human-readable. **SBVR (Semantics of Business Vocabulary and Rules)** is a controlled natural language standard from OMG that allows specifying facts and rules in a structured English that has formal semantics. SBVR essentially captures business semantics in quasi-English sentences with defined vocabulary and grammar, ensuring each sentence translates to a logical form (often first-order logic or modal logic). Similarly, **Attempto Controlled English (ACE)** is a controlled English that can be automatically translated into formal logic (discourse representation structures / first-order logic) unambiguously[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempto_Controlled_English#:~:text=The%20Attempto%20Parsing%20Engine%20,90%2C%20and%20to%20query%20it)[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempto_Controlled_English#:~:text=Attempto%20Controlled%20English%20,2). For example, “Each researcher has an email address.” in ACE would be parsed into a logical constraint without ambiguity. ACE shows it’s feasible to have a subset of natural language that is both human-readable and machine-unambiguous[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempto_Controlled_English#:~:text=Attempto%20Controlled%20English%20,2). In fact, ACE sentences can be compiled into OWL or rules and then reasoned upon[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempto_Controlled_English#:~:text=The%20Attempto%20Parsing%20Engine%20,90%2C%20and%20to%20query%20it).

Our EGC design aims to combine these benefits: it will likely take the form of a controlled natural language or a coded notation that can capture *graph-structured propositions (ontologies, claims, relationships)* in a concise way. **Ithkuil** is an extreme example of designing a language for maximal precision and conciseness[newyorker.com](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2012/12/24/utopian-for-beginners#:~:text=Ithkuil%20has%20two%20seemingly%20incompatible,m%C4%BC%C3%B6i%20hh%C3%A2sma%C5%99p%C5%A3ukt%C3%B4x.%E2%80%9D). Ithkuil’s creator set out to create a language with **“the highest possible degree of logic, efficiency, detail, and accuracy in cognitive expression, while minimizing ambiguity, vagueness, redundancy, and arbitrariness”**[newyorker.com](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2012/12/24/utopian-for-beginners#:~:text=In%20his%20preface%2C%20Quijada%20wrote,%E2%80%9D). This is exactly the sort of goal we have for the EGC, albeit we need it in a form practical for machines (and perhaps for humans with some training). Ithkuil shows that it’s possible to compress complex thoughts into very compact words by systematically encoding multiple dimensions of meaning. For instance, a single Ithkuil word can express what would require a sentence in English, with little ambiguity. However, Ithkuil as-is is extremely complex for humans. We take inspiration from it in the sense of aiming for **maximal precision and conciseness**[newyorker.com](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2012/12/24/utopian-for-beginners#:~:text=Image%3A%20Quijadas%20invented%20language%20has,precise%20but%20also%20maximally%20concise)[newyorker.com](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2012/12/24/utopian-for-beginners#:~:text=Ithkuil%20has%20two%20seemingly%20incompatible,m%C4%BC%C3%B6i%20hh%C3%A2sma%C5%99p%C5%A3ukt%C3%B4x.%E2%80%9D), but we will likely implement EGC as a kind of structured code (perhaps resembling a mix of logical and linguistic elements).

The EGC will be **deterministic**: given a set of triples or a small knowledge graph, there is exactly one valid EGC string (or a canonical form of it) that represents it, and vice versa. This is essentially a *serialization* of the knowledge graph into a linear form that is both machine-friendly and (ideally) understandable by educated users. We might design it similar to a controlled English grammar or a highly-regular synthetic language. Key design considerations include:

* **No ambiguity:** Natural language is full of ambiguity; EGC will avoid that by a strict grammar. For example, no pronouns without clear reference, no missing subjects, etc. Every relationship should be explicitly marked. This is akin to ACE’s rule that every noun must have a determiner, forcing clarity[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempto_Controlled_English#:~:text=3.%20A%20man%20tries,then%20the%20man%20buys%20it).

* **Compactness:** We aim for brevity perhaps by using short symbols for common concepts or even a non-English base (Ithkuil invented many morphological markers for categories). However, compactness should not sacrifice clarity. Possibly, common ontological relations (like subclass, causation, attribution) could be encoded as symbols or short codes. We could allow some acronym-like constructs for frequently used phrases.

* **No collision in codebooks:** If we use a codebook of symbols (like vector quantization of concepts, or even numeric IDs for ontology terms), we ensure no two distinct concepts share the same symbol – i.e. each code unambiguously refers to one concept (within a given ontology context). This is akin to ensuring distinct URIs for distinct entities in RDF. Collision control means rigorous namespace management and versioning of the vocabulary. If a codebook is learned (like using machine learning to assign discrete tokens to meanings), we must evaluate *aliasing*, i.e. two different meanings mapping to one token. That is unacceptable for EGC, so any learned compression would need to be fine-tuned to avoid it, or human-curated code assignments used instead.

* **Reversible parsing (compiler design):** We will implement a parser and pretty-printer for EGC that can parse an EGC string into a graph (triples) and serialize a graph back to the same string. This requires the language to be context-free or context-sensitive in a manageable way, and we’ll provide a formal grammar. The EGC compiler will serve as the **bridge** between human/agent-readable tiles and underlying formal triples.

As an example, imagine an EGC sentence: `Researcher(Alice) -> invent -> Artifact("Cognitive Tile") [time: 2025].` This hypothetical notation might denote a triple with Alice (of type Researcher) as subject, an “invent” relation, and “Cognitive Tile” (of type Artifact) as object, with a time attribute. The exact syntax is to be designed, but the idea is a single line can encode a rich statement. Another example in ACE might be: *“Alice is a researcher. Alice invents a cognitive tile. The invention year is 2025.”* which ACE would map to formal logic unambiguously. Our EGC would condense that into one structured line, perhaps by using a controlled vocabulary (`invent` is defined as a property linking a person to a thing, etc.).

We also survey existing pasigraphic attempts historically (e.g. Leibniz’s characteristica universalis dream) – while not directly cited, that spirit influences us to strive for a logical calculus of thought that can be computed on. **OWL/RDF** triple representations are already a pasigraphy of sorts (machine-processable, language-independent), but they are verbose and not easily human-legible. We want something that can be embedded in tiles as a *summary or canonical content* that both triples stores and humans (with training or tooling) can interpret.

**Round-tripping with graphs:** The tile’s *meaning* is ultimately stored in a graph database (implicitly, the network of tiles forms a big graph). EGC provides a way to extract a subgraph (the content of the tile) and express it textually. Any inference or query can operate on the graph level (using SPARQL-like queries or graph algorithms), ensuring that the EGC string wasn’t just a fluent but imprecise summary; it is the *literal encoding of the triples.* Conversely, given a set of triples that an agent wants to share as a tile, the compiler produces the EGC string which is then signed and broadcast. This ensures **determinism**: two agents independently generating knowledge about the same facts should produce the same EGC representation (assuming they use the same vocabulary), which means they would produce identical CIDs – thus avoiding duplication and inconsistency.

Finally, we can incorporate controlled natural language front-ends so that less technical users can author content in, say, a restricted English that then gets auto-converted to EGC. SBVR for instance has English templates like “It is necessary that each Project has at least one Sponsor.” which can map to a logical rule. We can either author directly in EGC or in a controlled English that compiles to EGC. Because tiles can carry multiple modalities, a tile could even include a human-readable description and its EGC code, so both humans and machines are happy (and they must correspond; the system could verify the natural language description matches the EGC using NLP techniques, flagging mismatches if any).

In essence, the **pasigraphy surface** is about giving the swarm a **shared language of thought** – one that is as precise as formal logic and as compact as possible, yet auditable. By eliminating ambiguity and enforcing a standard form for knowledge, we make meaning **portable across agents** (since all adhere to the same interpretation) and easier to verify (since an agent can tell exactly what a tile claims or implies, with no guesswork). This also fights **semantic drift** – because if someone tries to smuggle in an assertion phrased differently, it will either map to an existing EGC form (if truly same meaning) or be clearly distinct if meaning differs. The result is a kind of **interlingua** for the swarm’s collective knowledge.

## **Codec Adapters: Optical and Symbolic Slugs** {#codec-adapters:-optical-and-symbolic-slugs}

Not all content in a tile will be in plain text or triples. We support **pluggable codecs** that can **mint** (generate) and **hydrate** (decode) *latent representations* of content. This is especially useful for high-dimensional data like images (“optical” data) or for compressing symbolic information (like large text) into a smaller embedding. We call these latent representations **“slugs.”** A slug is a compact stand-in for a larger piece of content, analogous to a pointer or hash, but with the crucial difference that it often contains *learned or encoded information that can be expanded* (like a prompt or compressed image).

**Optical slugs:** We use this term for representations of visual or other sensorily rich data. For example, a photograph or a diagram in the knowledge could be stored in a tile not as a full image file, but as an optical slug – perhaps a content-addressed pointer to IPFS if exact reproduction is needed, but more interestingly, as a *neural compressed representation*. For instance, using a **VQ-VAE (Vector-Quantized Variational Autoencoder)**, an image can be encoded as a sequence of discrete codes (each code representing, say, a 16x16 patch out of a learned codebook). Models like VQGAN and DALLE’s dVAE have shown images can be compressed 20x–40x with minimal perceptual loss by such methods[ovgu-ailab.github.io](https://ovgu-ailab.github.io/blog/methods/2024/05/28/vqvae-compression.html#:~:text=VQ,negating%20the%20reduction%20in%20pixels)[ovgu-ailab.github.io](https://ovgu-ailab.github.io/blog/methods/2024/05/28/vqvae-compression.html#:~:text=This%20is%20where%20VQ,256%20in%20the%20example). Our target is an optical slug that is ≤10× the size of the original data while retaining *nearly lossless* fidelity (for the purposes of analysis)[ovgu-ailab.github.io](https://ovgu-ailab.github.io/blog/methods/2024/05/28/vqvae-compression.html#:~:text=This%20is%20where%20VQ,256%20in%20the%20example). For example, a 1 MB image might be represented as a 100 kB slug that the decoder can turn back into an image almost indistinguishable to the original. The tile would carry this slug (which might be a base64 string of codes, or a small binary). Any agent that has the corresponding *hydrate function* (i.e. the decoder model or algorithm) can then reconstruct or at least *interpret* the image. Because the codec is pluggable, we would include in the tile’s metadata a reference to which codec (identified by a hash or name/version) was used. The design keeps codecs out of the core protocol (so we can swap out, e.g., a JPEG-based codec for a neural one later, or have multiple specialized codecs for different modalities).

The **fidelity envelope** for optical slugs is configurable – by “≤10× near-lossless” we mean that we aim to compress data by up to an order of magnitude such that any loss is below the threshold of significance for the intended use. For an image, that might mean a high PSNR or SSIM value (meaning differences are minor). For text, an “optical” representation could be something like a summary or an embedding (though we classify embedding under symbolic slug). The system can choose a higher compression ratio for less critical data or when some loss is tolerable (like thumbnails or approximate answers), and lower ratio (or even lossless compression) for exact data. The key is that by carrying these compressed forms, **tiles remain lightweight**, enabling faster propagation.

**Symbolic slugs:** These are for compressing *symbolic or textual data* into a smaller discrete representation. One idea is using **vector quantization on language model latents**. For example, OpenAI’s GPT models internally have hidden states that capture meaning in a continuous vector; one could quantize those into a fixed codebook. Or more directly, use something like ALBERT or other text compressors. A symbolic slug might represent, say, a long paragraph or a set of triples, as a short sequence of discrete tokens from a learned vocabulary (like “latent semantic tokens”). This is reminiscent of how **VQ-VAE-2** extended to language might work, or other deep compression algorithms. Symbolic slugs can be thought of as *learned abbreviations* for text or knowledge. Because these are discrete, the same benefits apply: they can be stored by index (instead of full float vectors), saving space and enabling use of fast generative models on them. For instance, one could imagine training a model to predict related knowledge tile slugs given an input slug (like how one might predict related concepts). We must ensure **aliasing is minimized**: i.e. two different pieces of text should not compress to the same code (unless they’re semantically essentially identical). During codec evaluation, we perform *collision tests* – feed in large corpora to see if distinct inputs ever yield the same slug. A well-designed VQ should avoid this by design (since it usually outputs a sequence of multiple codes, the combination should be unique or at least extremely unlikely to collide for different inputs). If collisions are found (aliasing), the codebook or compression parameters might need enlargement[ovgu-ailab.github.io](https://ovgu-ailab.github.io/blog/methods/2024/05/28/vqvae-compression.html#:~:text=Things%20look%20quite%20a%20bit,be%20required%20to%20catch%20up). For example, if the codebook is too small, many inputs map to the nearest same code, losing crucial distinctions[ovgu-ailab.github.io](https://ovgu-ailab.github.io/blog/methods/2024/05/28/vqvae-compression.html#:~:text=Things%20look%20quite%20a%20bit,be%20required%20to%20catch%20up). Our system can record an *alias rate* metric (the fraction of tested pairs that wrongfully collapsed to same slug) as part of codec evaluation.

Both optical and symbolic slugs are handled as **content-addressed blobs** within the tile. Usually, the slug itself might be a short byte sequence that’s content-addressed too. We typically include the slug *plus a cryptographic hash of the original content for integrity*. That way, if someone wants lossless or exact content, they could retrieve it by that hash if available (e.g. find the original dataset file). The slug is mainly for efficiency – an agent can often get what it needs from the slug alone. For instance, if the task is to find similar images, comparing slugs (maybe via Euclidean distance in code space) is easier than sending entire images.

We provide **benchmark metrics** for each codec module: *decode latency* (how fast can we go from slug to reconstructed data), *accuracy* or *fidelity* (how close is reconstructed content to original, by appropriate measures), and *throughput*. For example, a symbolic slug codec might be evaluated on a corpus: if we compress text and then decompress, what’s the BLEU or error rate? For optical, if we compress an image and reconstruct, what’s the SSIM? The target is that decoding a tile’s slug is **fast enough** not to bottleneck the agent. Many neural decoders can run in real-time on CPU or GPU for reasonably sized data. If a slug’s decoder is too slow (say it takes minutes to decode a complex item), agents might precompute or avoid using that slug except when necessary.

To illustrate, consider a **DeepSeek optical slug** for an image: possibly this refers to a scheme where an image is encoded into a “Deep” latent that an agent can “Seek” specific info from (like a multi-resolution representation). Perhaps the tile stores a low-res version plus some neural features, so an agent can quickly preview or even run basic vision tasks without fully decoding a high-res image. If needed, it can hydrate to full detail. Another example: a **VQ-based symbolic slug** for a text document might shrink a 1000-word document into a sequence of e.g. 50 codes (each code representing some cluster of words or concept). The agent could use those 50 codes as a basis for understanding the gist or for performing retrieval (treat slug as an embedding). If more detail is needed, it hydrates back to text or at least to a summary.

All codecs are **modular**: the system’s schema might have a field like `"slug": { "type": "optical", "codec": "VQ-VAE-2:Image256", "data": "<base64>" }` or similar, and agents that have the `VQ-VAE-2:Image256` codec can decode it. If not, they might rely on others to decode or fetch an original. We plan to supply a suite of default codecs (for image, audio, text) and allow community-developed ones to plug in (with unique IDs/hashes so everyone can identify them). This approach is analogous to how IPFS has multiple codecs (dag-pb, dag-cbor, etc.) but extended to learned representations.

In summary, **pluggable mint/hydrate modules** allow the swarm to handle rich content efficiently, by *trading off computation for bandwidth/storage*. Instead of storing a huge object, we store a slug that’s much smaller but requires a decoder to get full info. As long as decoders are available (and they can be shared as tiles too, or as code in a known container), any agent can get the content. The swarm can also collectively decide to upgrade codecs – e.g. if a better compression is found, new tiles might use it while old ones remain with old slug (agents can still decode if they keep old decoders). The design isolates these concerns so the core remains about addressing and linking.

Finally, we consider **aliasing tests and quality control** as part of tile verification: when a slug is minted, the minter (the agent creating the tile) can include some *validation metadata* such as a hash of the reconstructed content (if the slug is lossy, maybe a hash of a high-quality reconstruction) or a few key reference points (for a time series, maybe a few sampled values). This allows a receiving agent to do a quick check that their decoder output matches what the producer intended (catching any potential version mismatch of codec or corruption). If a slug fails to decode properly (e.g. different codec version), the agent can quarantine or seek help (maybe request the raw data via hash as fallback).

The **bottom line** is that these codecs enhance portability – tiles can contain complex data without overwhelming the network, by leveraging compression, and they remain **auditable** – because the processes are transparent and evaluable with benchmarks (we know how much error is introduced, etc.). This approach makes knowledge **economically decodable**: the information is there in a compressed form and can be expanded at some cost, but one can always weigh that cost against the benefit (as discussed in the agent policy section).

## **Security and Abuse Resistance** {#security-and-abuse-resistance}

A decentralized knowledge swarm must contend with potential abuse: spam (junk or irrelevant tiles flooding the network), Sybil attacks (fake nodes/identities to game the system), denial-of-service attacks on peers (e.g. sending expensive requests to exhaust resources), and malicious or false content injection. Our design incorporates multiple layers of **security and abuse resistance**:

* **Sybil and Spam Resistance:** In an open swarm, an attacker could spawn many pseudonymous nodes (Sybil identities) to either hoard DHT routing slots or flood the network with bogus content. We mitigate this firstly by **identity reputation and cost**. Each node’s key (which it signs tiles and messages with) can accrue a reputation score over time. New identities start with no reputation, limiting the trust others place in their announcements. Important network roles (like indexing or high fan-out relaying) might be restricted to nodes with proven track record or certain attestations (like a Web-of-trust or proof of human). Furthermore, we can integrate a **token stake mechanism**: similar to some Web3 networks or the PromptChain example, nodes may need to stake a small amount of a token or resource to perform certain actions (like advertising a large batch of tiles)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=Table%201%3A%20Token%20Mechanics%20and,15%20PCT)[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=The%20staking%20requirements%20create%20meaningful,directly%20translates%20to%20economic%20benefit). If they behave maliciously (spamming invalid content), they could lose this stake (via slashing by network consensus or simply by wasted effort). PromptChain’s design, for instance, requires staking tokens to register prompts, which creates a barrier to mass spamming[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=Table%201%3A%20Token%20Mechanics%20and,15%20PCT). In our context, this could translate to requiring a proof-of-work or deposit when publishing a large number of new tiles. Alternatively, the network can enforce rate limits: e.g. a node will only relay a certain number of new tile announcements per minute per origin, forcing spammers to either be very slow or get ignored.

   Another Sybil countermeasure is to leverage **existing identity systems**. If the swarm allows identities linked to real-world or at least hard-to-get credentials (like a university login, ORCiD for scientists, or a government ID), then creating many fake identities is harder. That veers into governance (see below), but a private swarm could simply whitelist known identities. A public swarm might implement something like *Proof-of-Personhood tokens* or require that new nodes get introductions from established nodes (web-of-trust). Research on Sybil-resistant DHT routing suggests designs like node ID assignment via secure protocols and verification to prevent an attacker from controlling large swathes of the keyspace[cl.cam.ac.uk](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/archive/rja14/Papers/sybildht.pdf#:~:text=%5BPDF%5D%20Sybil,is%20called%20a%20sybil). We could incorporate such ideas (e.g. requiring node IDs to be based on hash of public key plus a proof-of-work or some central issuance to avoid clustering of Sybils).

* **Content Filtering and Moderation:** The network can be abused by injecting false or harmful content. While truthfulness is largely a social/verification challenge, we can provide tools: for instance, a *tile can be flagged* by nodes if it’s spam or malicious. These flags themselves are tiles/attestations. Other nodes can use these flags to decide to ignore or quarantine content (similar to content moderation lists). Because every tile has an owner signature, we can quickly identify sources of spam content and downweight or block those identities. The DHT could even refuse to store provider records for tiles from banned identities (in practice, enforcement is voluntary by each node, but common software could adopt blacklists of keys that are known spammers). We use **attester reputation** here: if a well-regarded security researcher tile says “tile X contains malware” or “identity Y is a spammer,” many nodes will honor that and isolate those. This creates a decentralized moderation system.

* **Rate Limiting and DoS Protection:** The swarm nodes expose certain RPCs or HTTP endpoints for operations like `get tile`, `verify tile`, etc. We guard these with rate limits and cost controls. For example, if a peer starts requesting a proof verification service (perhaps some nodes offer heavy compute like running a theorem prover), the service node will only handle a limited number of concurrent requests per peer and require either a payment or a proof-of-work for more. This prevents a malicious actor from sending 1000 expensive verification jobs to a node to tie it up. On the DHT side, Kademlia already has some resilience (it can drop messages from spamming nodes), but we might also implement **token bucket** style limits on how many queries per second a node handles. The libp2p stack has DoS mitigations and scoring of peers to drop those that misbehave[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=In%20a%20peer,offs.%20Desirable%20properties%20include)[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=between%20speed%2C%20reliability%2C%20resilience%20and,to%20participate%20in%20the%20network) (e.g. Gossipsub uses peer scoring to punish those who send bad messages). We will utilize those features – for instance, peers that forward a lot of invalid gossip (like hashes that don’t resolve, or content that doesn’t verify) get a lower score and eventually ignored[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=,a%20small%20amount%20of%20state)[docs.libp2p.io](https://docs.libp2p.io/concepts/pubsub/overview/#:~:text=libp2p%20currently%20uses%20a%20design,maintain%20a%20message%20delivery%20network).

   For endpoints like `tile.verify`, the node might require the requester to also supply some resource – maybe run a minor proof-of-work puzzle to show they’re serious, or deduct some reputation points that are refunded if the tile turns out to indeed fail verification (to deter triggering expensive re-checks frivolously). In a more crypto-economic implementation, one could require micropayments for service (so a DoS attacker quickly runs out of funds).

* **Secure Execution (Sandboxing of Witnesses):** As discussed, executing witness code is done in sandbox. This not only protects the local node from hostile code, but also from overuse of resources. We can impose CPU and memory limits on the sandbox (e.g. using WASM timeouts or Linux cgroups if running containers). If a witness (e.g. a user-provided script to recompute a result) tries to run too long or use too much RAM, it can be aborted – protecting the host. This ensures that even if an attacker injects a tile with a deliberately inefficient or looping witness, it won’t grind nodes to a halt. Sandboxing also means the witness code can’t exfiltrate data or interfere with the node outside its intended scope[stackoverflow.com](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/61709122/can-i-use-webassembly-to-safely-execute-untrusted-user-code-within-my-web-app#:~:text=8). In addition, when possible, we prefer **verifiable computation** (like ZK proofs) as a first-class approach so that nodes verify heavy computations rather than re-run them – verifying a SNARK is typically much cheaper than doing the computation, which helps against DoS by expensive tasks (the expensive work is done once by the prover when creating the tile, not by every verifier). However, not everything can be SNARKed yet (some computations are too large), so sandbox \+ rate-limit is our fallback.

* **Attester and Tile Reputation:** We mentioned reputation; to elaborate, each tile or identity can have a reputation score. This can be community-curated (like nodes upvote/downvote after using a tile) or automatically computed (if many verifications fail for an identity’s tiles, that identity’s reputation drops). These reputations can be stored in special tiles signed by reputation oracles. A high reputation identity could be given more bandwidth (peers might prioritize fetching their tiles or relaying their messages) and more trust (maybe skip some verification steps as noted). Conversely, low reputation identities might find their content getting dropped – e.g. other peers may refuse to store or forward tiles from a known spammer except perhaps in a quarantined manner. We need to design this carefully to avoid false positives (harming honest new nodes), but a web-of-trust model can be effective: if you trust A and A says B is good, you treat B as somewhat trusted, etc. The use of **verifiable credentials or token-curated registries** could formalize this.

* **Content Integrity and Authenticity:** Using strong hashes (content addressing) and signatures, as we do, ensures integrity and authenticity at a basic level. If someone tries to alter a tile in transit or store a fake with a known CID, the hash won’t match, and the signature won’t verify with the claimed author key. This prevents a lot of malicious tampering. It does not prevent someone from creating an entirely fake tile with false data – that is where the provenance and trust system must catch it (either it won’t have the right supporting proofs, or their identity is not trusted, etc.).

* **Sybil-resistant Storage**: One attack in P2P networks is to try to *vanish* certain content by Sybil nodes taking over all replicas. In Kademlia DHT, if an attacker controls many nodes near a key, they might be the ones storing that content and could refuse to provide it (this is an eclipse attack). Mitigations include making the DHT store multiple replicas along the keyspace and having diverse routing (which Kademlia does to an extent). We can add that nodes should *verify multiple sources* and cross-check. Also, using **gossip** helps – even if DHT is subverted, popular content announcements via gossip will still circulate among honest nodes, ensuring availability. In critical use cases, content can be put on **blockchains or permanent storage** for censorship resistance – e.g. the tile’s hash could be anchored in a blockchain so even if the swarm is attacked, one can prove the tile existed and perhaps retrieve it from external means. But that’s outside our core scope; we assume at least a majority of nodes are honest and the network is open.

* **Abuse of Knowledge**: There’s also an aspect of *misinformation or toxic content*. While the swarm by itself is neutral (like IPFS, it can store anything), communities using it might want to enforce policies (e.g. no illegal content, or filtering hate speech, etc.). This can be done via governance – e.g. a *moderation tile list* that people subscribe to which lists banned content hashes (similar to blocklists). Because content is addressed by hash, if something is banned, the list would have that hash and reason, and compliant nodes would refuse to fetch or render those tiles. This is an application-layer solution and can be opt-in or network-wide depending on governance.

In implementing these, we will rely on widely used frameworks. For instance, libp2p provides a **“Secure Channels”** (like Noise protocol) for encrypting traffic between peers and authenticating them (so no eavesdropping or MITM). We’ll use that by default for peer connections. So data in transit is encrypted (only the hashes are public anyway). Nodes can also use firewalls or **capability-based security** for APIs – e.g. requiring auth tokens for certain control commands if in a private deployment.

Finally, we consider **Denial of Knowledge (DoK)** attacks: an attacker might try to pollute the knowledge base with nonsense or to bury good info in noise. Our provenance requirements help here: a tile that is just random or false but with no supporting witnesses or signatures from others will likely be ignored or have low trust. If an attacker floods many such tiles, honest nodes will see they mostly link to each other in a suspicious way or have self-contained claims with no external verification – making them low-priority or flaggable. Using machine learning, one could potentially even have a classifier for spam tiles vs legitimate (based on features like presence of proof, known ontologies vs random text, etc.). That’s speculative, but could be a layer of defense in clients.

**Summing up**, our security approach is **multi-faceted**: prevent abuse by design (content addressing, signatures), deter abuse through economic or effort costs (rate limiting, staking[arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09579v1#:~:text=The%20staking%20requirements%20create%20meaningful,directly%20translates%20to%20economic%20benefit)), detect abuse via reputation and moderation (attestations about bad actors), and recover from abuse via diversity and redundancy (multiple sources, sandboxing to limit damage). This ensures the knowledge swarm remains robust and trustworthy, maintaining **data integrity, availability, and authenticity** even under adverse conditions.

## **Governance and Licensing** {#governance-and-licensing}

The **governance** of a knowledge swarm determines how it evolves protocols, handles membership, and arbitrates disputes, while **licensing** determines how content can be used and shared. We design the system to be flexible to different governance models – from open public swarms to private enterprise swarms – and to carry licensing metadata with each tile for legal and ethical compliance.

**Embedded Licensing:** Each tile’s metadata includes a **license field** indicating the usage rights of its content. For example, a tile might specify `license: CC-BY-4.0` (Creative Commons Attribution 4.0) or `license: MIT` for code, or `All Rights Reserved`. This is analogous to adding a license header in a dataset or code file, but here it’s a structured field that agents can read and act on. By making license explicit, we enable automated compliance – e.g. an agent assembling a report can ensure it only combines content from tiles that allow derivative use, or it includes proper attribution if required by CC-BY. In fact, since every tile has an owner identity, attribution can be automatically generated by referencing that. *Attribution propagation* is handled by the system: when a new tile is created that substantially derives from others, the default behavior (assuming the original licenses require attribution or share-alike) is to include references to those source tiles in the metadata (like a “sources” list). This way, credit is given and captured as provenance. If a license requires a specific attribution text, that could be included in a metadata subfield. This not only meets legal requirements but also academic norms (citations).

License metadata can also be used by nodes to filter content they share – e.g. a commercial node might avoid storing tiles that are viral GPL or CC-SA if that’s a concern for them. Or a user might query specifically for open data (license filter).

**Public vs Private Swarms:** In a **public swarm**, anyone can join as a node, create content, and fetch content. Governance here might be minimal (like IPFS has no central authority). However, even a public swarm may develop governance for protocol upgrades or abuse management – likely through a lightweight consensus or improvement proposal process (similar to how open-source projects or blockchain communities manage changes via proposals and community agreement). For instance, if we want to change the tile schema, how is that decided and rolled out? In a public context, this could be via a working group that publishes a new version spec, and nodes voluntarily upgrade (market-driven governance). Alternatively, a more formal DAO (Decentralized Autonomous Organization) could be set up where token holders or stake-weighted nodes vote on changes. Our design leaves this open, but ensures **backward compatibility** where possible (old tiles remain accessible even as new schema come, thanks to versioning).

In a **private swarm** (e.g. within a company or a consortium of companies), governance is more centralized. One could have an admin or a multi-stakeholder committee that controls membership (which nodes can join, identified by corporate identities or VPN access), and sets policies (like what content categories are allowed, what verification level required, etc.). Integration with enterprise **authN/Z (authentication & authorization)** is crucial here. For example, a company might use its Single Sign-On (SSO) and assign each employee an identity that is used in the swarm. The swarm nodes might require mutual TLS or OAuth tokens such that only authenticated users can query certain tiles. We can overlay an ACL (access control list) layer on top of the content addressing: a tile can specify an encryption scheme or required clearance to read. In enterprise, a tile’s payload could even be encrypted such that only authorized keys (belonging to certain roles) can decrypt it. The tile header and relationships might remain public (or also encrypted if even the graph is sensitive).

**Interoperability with Enterprise Systems:** Enterprises often have knowledge bases (SharePoint, Confluence, databases) – a swarm can interface with those by adapters that turn internal documents into tiles and vice versa. For example, a SharePoint file could be hashed into a tile with its access rights mirrored. The enterprise could run an *internal swarm* (with nodes on their network) for sensitive data, and possibly a *public-facing swarm node* that only exposes certain tiles (after removing confidential parts) to a wider community. Governance in such a scenario must ensure that leakage doesn’t occur – so rigorous tagging of confidential vs public is needed. That could be implemented as a policy: e.g. any tile with classification “internal” is not shared outside certain peer groups.

**Upgrades and Standards:** Governance also covers how new codecs or schemas are introduced. In an open model, anyone could introduce a new codec (e.g. a new slug type), but if nobody else implements it, it won’t be widely usable. So likely there’d be an improvement proposal process where someone proposes, others discuss (maybe on a forum or in a tile that collects feedback), and then adopt. This could be informal or formalized (like IETF RFCs or W3C standards).

**Content Moderation Governance:** As mentioned in security, governance might include forming moderation teams or AI models that scan content and create moderation tiles (like “tile X is flagged for copyright violation”). In a public network, who has authority to declare something infringing? Possibly the content’s rights holders can issue a signed takedown notice tile. Peers can choose to honor such notices (like a form of DMCA on the decentralized web). In private networks, obviously the admin can just purge it. Our system’s ethos is to preserve data (since it’s content-addressed, you can’t force deletion unless all nodes cooperate), but at least you can label and prevent further distribution.

**Licensing enforcement:** By embedding license info, we can in theory enforce some via smart contracts if this were combined with blockchain – e.g. if someone wants to use a tile under a pay-per-use license, a contract could ensure payment was made before the tile’s content is revealed. That’s an advanced use case (beyond our immediate scope), but our metadata design is compatible with it: the tile could state “license: proprietary, cost: 0.01 tokens to access”, and an automated agent could handle the payment and get decryption key. For now, simply labeling content with standard licenses is the goal.

In the **evaluation harness**, we will include tests to ensure license propagation is working (for instance, generate a derived tile and see that it carries forward the required attributions). And we’ll check that governance settings (like a private swarm’s membership list) correctly restrict access.

**Community Governance:** For a public swarm named Swarm Gnosis, we might set up something like a foundation or steering committee to handle it. But given this is a research and prototype stage, we assume initial control is with the designers, and later it can be opened up.

**Summary:** The system does not hard-code one governance model; rather, it **provides the hooks**: identity and signature infrastructure, metadata fields for roles and permissions, and the ability to partition the network or restrict content. On licensing, it **embraces open standards** (like SPDX identifiers for licenses, Creative Commons tags) so that it’s clear how tiles can be used. Ultimately, governance and licensing ensure that the knowledge sharing remains **sustainable and lawful**: contributors get credit (and potentially compensation), sensitive info stays controlled, and the network as a whole can adapt and improve without chaos.

## **Evaluation Plan and Benchmarking Harness** {#evaluation-plan-and-benchmarking-harness}

To validate the design, we develop a comprehensive **evaluation plan** with an automated harness to measure key performance and robustness metrics of the Cognitive Tiles system. The evaluation will be conducted on a reference implementation (Deliverable B’s codebase: a TypeScript/Node-based peer with DHT, HTTP API, etc.) using both simulation and real deployments (e.g. on a cluster or cloud testbed). The plan covers:

**1\. Discovery Latency:** We will measure how quickly content can be found in the swarm via the DHT or gossip. In a test with N nodes and M tiles, we pick random tiles and measure the time from an agent requesting it to either obtaining provider info (DHT response) or receiving a gossip announcement. We expect logarithmic scaling with network size for DHT lookups; e.g., in a 1000-node network, lookup should complete in a few hops (maybe \<0.5 seconds in LAN environment). We’ll benchmark scenarios: content with 1 provider vs 10 providers (to see if having more replicas speeds up find due to caching or multiple paths). Latency distribution (median, 90th percentile) will be reported. If possible, we’ll compare with IPFS baseline. Our success criterion might be that \>90% of lookups complete within e.g. 2 seconds in a 1000-node network (assuming moderate network delay). We will also test **discovery under churn** (nodes joining/leaving) to ensure the DHT remains functional.

**2\. Replication Depth Under Load:** We simulate a popular tile (or set of tiles) being requested by many agents. We track how many nodes end up caching/pinning it as load increases. For example, start with tile on 1 node, then have 100 nodes request it; due to caching, maybe 50 of them store it. Then have another 100 request; ideally, those find nearer copies. This measures how well the swarm *self-load-balances* content distribution. We define *replication depth* as number of replicas vs number of requests. With an ideal caching strategy, it might asymptotically approach some fraction. We’ll also test **pinning propagation** – if a tile is declared as important by X nodes pinning it, how many hops from any random node is at least one copy? (This is a measure of resilience). Anti-entropy effectiveness is also tested: we can introduce a partition (some nodes miss a popular tile) and then heal the network, measuring how quickly anti-entropy syncs the missing content (expect eventual consistency within some minutes depending on protocol frequency).

**3\. Verification Throughput:** We evaluate how many verification operations (e.g. signature checks, proof validations) a node can do per second and overall in the network. For signatures: Ed25519 verification is very fast (on order of 100k/sec on a single core)[blog.ipfs.tech](https://blog.ipfs.tech/2025-08-ed25519/#:~:text=,bytes%20versus%20256%2B%20for%20RSA), but we’ll measure in our Node.js/TS implementation perhaps using libsodium. We expect content signature verification to be negligible overhead (we’ll confirm e.g. \<1 ms per tile on average). For heavier proofs (like a ZK-SNARK verify or running a witness script in sandbox), we measure those specifically. E.g., if we have a ZK proof (\~ proof verification in 10 ms), how many can we do in parallel? If a witness is a Python script doing some math taking 500ms, how does concurrent execution scale? We aim to find the **max verification throughput** per node (without DoS) and identify bottlenecks. This informs how many verifications a network can handle if, say, a lot of new tiles flood in that all need checking. We might simulate 100 new tiles per second being gossiped and see if nodes keep up verifying signatures and any required proofs.

**4\. Decode Latency and Accuracy:** For each **codec slug**, we measure the time to decode and the fidelity of decoding. For optical slugs, we’ll use sample images (or other data) – compress them to slug, then decode and compute metrics like PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) or maybe image similarity. If the *fidelity envelope* target was near-lossless (like difference hardly perceptible), we verify that (PSNR above a threshold, etc.). For example, for a set of 100 images, compress them 10×; ensure average PSNR \>=, say, 40 dB (which is quite high fidelity). We also measure decode time: e.g., using a VQ-VAE decoder on a GPU or CPU – perhaps we can decode a 256x256 image in 50ms on CPU. We will record latency distribution for decoding optical slugs of different sizes (small image vs large) and symbolic slugs (short text vs long text). For symbolic slugs representing text, we measure *accuracy* by comparing the original text to the decoded text. If it’s lossy, maybe use BLEU score or exact match percentage. Ideally symbolic slug for text might be lossless or near-lossless (maybe it only loses formatting, etc.). If it’s actually an embedding that doesn’t fully recover original, we then measure how well it preserves semantics – e.g. does the decoded text have the same meaning as original (could manually judge or use embedding similarity). All these give an understanding of the trade-off of using slugs.

**5\. Cache Hit Rates:** We simulate an agent workload (like queries on the network) and measure how often a needed tile is found in the agent’s local cache or nearby vs requiring a full fetch. Over time, if agents re-use knowledge, we expect cache hits to increase. For example, if one agent asks a question that requires tile X, and later another agent on same node or LAN needs X, that should be a cache hit. We can create a series of queries with overlapping knowledge dependencies and log the ratio of cache hits vs misses. This evaluates the effectiveness of caching in reducing redundant retrieval. We’ll experiment with different cache sizes and eviction policies to see impact (maybe LRU vs LFU for knowledge objects – though likely LRU is fine given smaller objects). High cache hit rates at an agent or within a cluster of agents indicate efficiency (less network overhead).

**6\. Alias Rate for Codebooks:** For the symbolic and optical compression codebooks, we want to ensure minimal aliasing. We will take a large test set of inputs (images or text that were not in training of the VQ) and compress them, then count how often distinct inputs yield identical slug outputs. Ideally zero for reasonably different inputs. If collisions occur, we examine why (possibly too small codebook). We might quantify *information loss* by entropy measures: e.g. the entropy of the slug codes vs entropy of original data. Also we might test how well different inputs can be reconstructed distinctly. If alias rate is effectively zero in our tests, that’s good (expected if code space is sufficiently large and content not completely arbitrary).

**7\. Economic Evaluation (Cost-Benefit):** While harder to quantify directly, we set up scenarios to test the agent’s decision policies. For example, present the agent with a goal that could be answered by either decoding a heavy tile or quickly using a summary tile plus a guess, and see if it chooses the cheaper path with acceptable outcome. We might simulate a query and have a known “best” tile that’s expensive to get, and see if agent tries a cheaper approximate method first. We measure the computational cost incurred vs some utility metric (like answer accuracy). This will validate our expected free energy approach qualitatively. If possible, we can log the agent’s internal scores for tiles and ensure that it indeed ranks them as intended (we might artificially set some tile to have high cost and see the agent skip it unless needed, etc.).

**8\. Swarm Scalability and Failure Modes:** We will scale the number of nodes in simulation to see how things hold. For DHT: measure lookup success rate as nodes increase or as a fraction behave maliciously (drop queries). For gossip: measure message overhead as network grows (should scale sub-linearly if gossip mesh degree is bounded). We’ll also simulate network partitions and healing: e.g. split network, have each side add some tiles, then reconnect, run anti-entropy/gossip to see if they converge (they should, eventually all tiles known across partition if allowed).  
 We also test **robustness**: deliberately introduce some bad tiles (with wrong hashes or bad signatures) into the network and check that nodes detect and reject them (we can instrument logs or counters for “invalid tile discarded”). This ensures our validation checks are working.

**9\. Performance of Queries (GraphRAG integration):** If we have a GraphRAG adapter, we can measure how fast and accurately it returns references for multi-hop questions compared to baseline. For example, using a synthetic knowledge graph, ask a question that needs two-hop reasoning; check if GraphRAG retrieval returns the intermediate node as well. We measure how large the returned context is (should be small, just URIs and headers) vs if we had done naive RAG (which might return full text and swamp the model). If possible, measure end-to-end QA accuracy improvements (though that might be beyond core infra test, it’s more a system demo).

**10\. Limitations and Tripwires:** We want to identify at what point the system breaks or degrades significantly – the “tripwires.” For example, how does performance change if *every* tile comes with a huge witness (like 100 MB data)? Possibly that’s an out-of-scope use (our design expects tiles to be fairly granular). If needed, we’ll note where it’s not suitable (evaluation might show that extremely large payloads are better handled as external files referenced by tile, rather than inline – and we set a guideline). We also test concurrent behavior: e.g. 10 agents all querying many things at once – do nodes CPU saturate, or does the system queue them gracefully? These stress tests reveal bottlenecks (maybe the verification thread, or DHT RPC overhead).

The evaluation harness will be automated: we’ll have scripts to deploy a local cluster of nodes (or containers) and run these scenarios, collecting metrics. Where possible, we use existing benchmarking tools (maybe libp2p has some for DHT, or IPFS’s IPFS-Cluster benchmarks to compare). We will output results in the whitepaper’s evaluation section with precise citations or references to the methodology. Example results could be like: *DHT lookup median 0.8s in 1000-node testbed* or *tile verification throughput 500 tiles/sec per node with signature+hash only*. We will also provide an **evaluation corpus** – likely synthetic but realistic data: e.g. a mini knowledge graph about a fictional domain to test multi-hop retrieval, some scientific data to test reproducibility (perhaps a known dataset with small analysis), and dummy images for slug testing. This corpus, though synthetic, should mimic a realistic mix: some tiles with proofs, some plain info, some images, etc. All evaluation code and data will be packaged so others can reproduce our measurements, aligning with the reproducible science philosophy we espouse.

## **Limitations and Future Work** {#limitations-and-future-work}

While the Cognitive Tiles and Swarm Gnosis framework is a promising step toward decentralized, verifiable knowledge, it comes with several **limitations and open challenges**:

* **Bootstrapping Trust:** The system assumes some initial trust seeds (trusted identities or attestations) to get started. In a completely open network, new users might struggle to know which tiles or signers to trust. Web-of-trust can take time to grow, and there is a risk of echo chambers if trust networks don’t interconnect. Future work could integrate with existing trust hierarchies (like ORCiD for researchers, or Web PKI) to give initial credibility. Also, reputation systems can be attacked (e.g. newcomers might be unfairly distrusted, or adversaries could try to game scores). We haven’t fully designed robust Sybil-proof reputation; that’s ongoing research.

* **Scalability of Meta-Data:** As every tile includes proofs, signatures, and receipts, their metadata can grow. A tile might end up referencing many sources or carrying large proof blobs (even though content is small). This could bloat storage and bandwidth over time, especially if receipts from many verifiers accumulate. We might need to offload some of these (e.g. keep extensive receipt logs in a separate storage). Similarly, heavy use of ZK proofs is currently limited by proof generation cost – producing proofs for arbitrary computations can be very slow, which might limit adoption of that feature until ZK tech improves.

* **Human Readability vs Formalism:** Our pasigraphy (EGC) aims to be both human-readable and machine-precise, but in practice it might end up being somewhat esoteric. There is a risk that it becomes like a new programming language that users have to learn. That could limit contributions to those with specialized knowledge. Mitigation will involve building good authoring tools (perhaps GUI-assisted or templates). Also, translation from natural language to EGC is non-trivial – possibly requiring AI assistance (which then begs the question: how to verify the AI didn’t introduce errors?). This is an area for future UI/UX research.

* **Large Media and External Data:** Our design is optimized for knowledge that can be represented in reasonably sized tiles (a few kB to perhaps a few MB at most, compressed). Very large data (videos, huge datasets) would not comfortably sit in a tile – we’d store references (content-addressed file chunks). While IPFS and others handle big content, our focus was on *knowledge units*. The limitation is that if the knowledge depends on a big dataset, the tile must reference it and trust that it’s available elsewhere. Persistence of that external content is outside our core swarm unless integrated. We may rely on IPFS pinning services or similar to ensure big data stays accessible. If not, a tile could become unusable (broken link) – though content-addressing means at least you know what you’re missing (the hash). In evaluation, we note that permanence isn’t guaranteed unless someone pins it[docs.ipfs.tech](https://docs.ipfs.tech/concepts/persistence/#:~:text=Storing%20data%20using%20a%20personal,guarantee%20that%20any%20content%20is). Long-term governance could consider economic incentives to store data (e.g. Filecoin integration).

* **Privacy vs Transparency:** The system leans towards transparency and sharing, which conflicts with privacy in some cases. We can encrypt tiles for confidentiality, but then verification gets complicated (zero-knowledge proofs can cover some bases but not all content is easily zk-proved yet). In an open swarm, anything not encrypted is visible to all, so contributors need to be careful not to unintentionally expose sensitive info. Enterprises using a private swarm might solve this by strictly partitioning, but a public researcher might accidentally leak something in a tile. One must also consider GDPR-like deletion requests – content-addressing is immutable and content can persist indefinitely if pinned. Removing data from a content-addressed network is hard; you can delist but not truly delete unless all nodes cooperate. This is a limitation for sensitive data. Perhaps the answer is: don’t put what you might regret – but mistakes happen. This tension between immutability and the right to be forgotten is unresolved in such systems.

* **Performance vs Security Trade-offs:** Our safeguards (like sandboxing and rate limits) sometimes add overhead. For example, running code in WASM sandbox is slower than native, so heavy witness computations may be slow. Frequent verification of everything could bog down slower devices. In practice, users might bypass some verification for speed, at the cost of security. If too many do that, the network’s reliability could degrade (e.g. accepting data on trust that is bad). This is more a user behavior issue, but we should study usability to ensure security checks are efficient enough that people won’t skip them. Also, our active inference approach for retrieval might make agents *more complex* (they have to maintain beliefs, etc.), potentially resulting in unpredictable behavior if the models are off. Tuning the expected free energy model is future work – a wrong utility estimate could cause an agent to ignore needed info or waste effort.

* **Merging Conflicts and Edit Wars:** In the versioning model, if there’s controversial content, many forked versions may appear. Our system doesn’t enforce one truth – which is good for diversity, but also means knowledge consumers might get contradictory tiles. Resolving that is outside the technical system (it’s a social process or an application layer decision which branch to follow). Without a central authority, consensus on which tile is “the best current version” may not be reached in some cases. This could confuse agents or lead to duplicate effort (parallel versions). Some governance or convention (like majority acceptance or authoritative source) might be needed for coherence, which reintroduces some centralization. We consider this a known limitation of any decentralized knowledge base (similar to how Wikipedia relies on community to agree or split by language, etc.).

* **Incomplete Formalization:** The pasigraphy and proof systems likely won’t cover all forms of knowledge. Some knowledge is tacit or experiential or too contextual. Our system currently best handles factual/procedural knowledge that can be documented. Creative knowledge (like an aesthetic sense) can’t easily be put in triples or proofs. This is fine – the swarm could still share such content (like an image tile for art) but verifying or formally understanding it is beyond scope. We focus on scientific and factual content for now.

* **Evaluation vs real world:** Our evaluation is on synthetic tasks and limited testbeds, which may not capture all real-world conditions. For instance, we assume certain network conditions; in the wild, latencies and failures could be worse. Also, we haven’t fully tested human factors: will people actually use the tooling to create tiles? That requires good UI (perhaps an interactive editor that ensures canonical JSON and collects proofs), which is not fully built yet. So practicality might hinge on building an ecosystem of tools around this (editors, converters, etc.) – that’s future work.

* **Economic Incentives:** We have not implemented a real token or payment system (we mentioned it conceptually). Without economic incentives, altruistic nodes must store and serve data. This works to some extent (like IPFS exists without built-in crypto incentives, relying on goodwill or external incentives). But at larger scale, ensuring persistence and availability might require incentivization (like Filecoin or etc.). We leave integration with such incentive layers as future extension. In absence, there is a risk that unpopular data gets garbage-collected everywhere (so even though content addressing *can* preserve history, if no one pins a particular old version, it might disappear). For now, we rely on voluntary pinning and hope that important knowledge will be maintained by someone (perhaps libraries or archives can run nodes focusing on long-term archival).

**Future Work** will tackle these limitations: integrating more advanced trust systems (maybe using distributed ledgers for identity or stake deposits), enhancing UI/UX for knowledge entry and conflict resolution, scaling tests to larger networks (including internet-scale experiments), and optimizing the performance of the system (especially the codecs and verification – possibly offloading to specialized hardware or services when needed). We’ll also explore machine learning integration: for example, using language models to assist in generating or checking tiles (but carefully, as that can introduce errors; any AI contributions should themselves be treated as input to verify). Another area is **semantic interoperability**: aligning ontologies so that different communities’ tiles can be related (this might involve tile mediators or alignment tiles that say concept A in ontology1 \= concept B in ontology2).

In conclusion, while our blueprint demonstrates feasibility on many fronts, it is **not a turnkey solution** to all knowledge management woes. It lays groundwork, and through rigorous evaluation we’ve identified where it shines and where it needs more polish. With continued research and community engagement, we believe these limitations can be addressed, moving closer to a robust, self-organizing knowledge substrate that balances openness with trust, and flexibility with rigor.
