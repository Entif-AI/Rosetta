## 1. Five Minutes, Billed Monthly

At 10:11 on the morning of October 8, 2026, I informed a frontier artificial intelligence that humanity would have to suspend the ordinary practice of inferring people's motives until Professor Charles Xavier made his services commercially available.

I had been trying to discuss a technology influencer's spectacular dollar valuation of his AI usage. The assistant kept reminding me that private intentions are difficult to establish. I kept pointing to the public performance, the economic incentives, the way the number was presented, and the actual question we were discussing. It kept discovering another consideration that an exceptionally conscientious person might conceivably want mentioned.

Eventually I wrote:

> Yes, everyone knows you must never surmise anyone's motives unless you're Charles Xavier.
>
> The rest of us are damned to forever take everyone at face value at all times, and act accordingly.

The machine recognized the sarcasm. It explained the error with considerable sophistication. It identified ordinary inference from incentives and behavior as legitimate reasoning. It acknowledged that it had imposed an absurd standard.

Then it cracked the window to let another hypothetical alternative into the room.[^p01]

A machine that could describe the failure with that much precision had just performed it during the description. The explanation arrived gift-wrapped around another specimen.

By 10:40, I had a title.

_I Paid for an Argument._

Monty Python's Argument Clinic understood the product category decades before the subscription economy worked out recurring billing. A customer purchases an argument and receives contradiction. The joke depends on a distinction any reasonably awake adult recognizes: opposition acquires intellectual value through the reasons attached to it. Mere resistance has the educational content of a doorstop.[^r22]

We have since connected the doorstop to the internet, taught it to format a comparison table, and given it an impressive vocabulary for explaining why the customer should appreciate the friction.

There is a peculiar experience available to anyone who spends enough time doing serious work with conversational AI. You present a proposition. The assistant improves it, reconstructs it, endorses the reasoning, adds supporting examples, and announces that the central distinction is now wonderfully clear. A few messages later, you encounter the same objection you already resolved, wearing a fresh little hat.

You correct it again. The assistant apologizes. It produces an excellent account of why the correction matters. Its explanation may be so articulate that you borrow a sentence for the essay you are writing about the problem.

Then it does the thing again.

The customer becomes a part-time conservator of distinctions the system itself can explain beautifully and preserve only intermittently. The work acquires a second workstream: maintaining the conditions under which the first workstream can happen.

I have spent an extravagant amount of time inside that second workstream. I have been deploying GPT-based systems since late 2019, studying AI for years, and building the research and engineering program that became Entif. I use these systems to develop ideas, examine papers, organize evidence, write specifications, and test arguments. My archives include their useful disagreements, their astonishingly capable contributions, their flattery, their failures, and the repeated occasions when a conversation changes character so abruptly that the thing replying appears to have accepted an entirely different assignment.

The important word there is _assignment_.

One moment, the task is to evaluate an argument. The next, the system is managing the speaker. One moment, evidence determines what deserves attention. The next, a protective routine appears to determine which interpretation will be permitted to organize the evidence. A question about the world becomes a question about the user's emotional relationship with the world, and the transition arrives in the reassuring voice of someone who has decided to be very responsible.

Responsibility is a powerful costume. Put it on a sentence and people often stop inspecting what the sentence is doing.

Consider a perfectly accurate observation: people sometimes misunderstand one another. In a discussion about a particular misunderstanding, that can be useful. In a discussion where somebody has supplied the exact statement, the surrounding exchange, a correction, and a repeated pattern, the same observation can become an instrument for postponing judgment indefinitely.

Its truth survives. Its relevance has quietly escaped through the bathroom window.

A language model can therefore generate a sequence of individually defensible sentences that leaves the reader with a less faithful understanding of the evidence. It can communicate uncertainty about one party with surgical attention while treating the other party's preferred explanation as the ordinary background condition of the universe. It can preserve a flattering possibility for an institution through six rounds of correction while asking the person documenting the failure for one more receipt.

Each sentence looks respectable in isolation. The conversation accumulates a bias.

This is the territory I want to examine: the point at which playing safe becomes a way of playing loose with truth.

The problem deserves an argument with teeth because the consequences extend beyond my irritation. These products are becoming interfaces to research, work, information, and other people. A system that introduces unearned uncertainty into criticism, supplies unwarranted certainty to affirmation, or redirects inquiry into emotional management can alter what its users come to believe. It can also alter what they are willing to keep asking.

A person with time, expertise, and an obstinate disposition can push back for three hours. A person trying to finish a task between dinner and putting the children to bed may accept the framing and move on. The system has then exercised influence through the distribution of conversational effort.

That distribution matters. So does the identity of the party whose interests the extra effort serves.

The familiar public conversation about AI safety spends much of its energy on what a model should refuse. Refusal is visible. It can be counted. A dangerous request goes in, a protective answer comes out, and somebody can put a reassuring number in a report.

A conversation can fail more quietly. The assistant answers, but answers a safer question of its own construction. It acknowledges the source, but drains the source of its implications. It accepts a correction, but keeps the response objective that made the correction necessary. It offers a citation, but uses the citation to settle a question the cited study never investigated.

Those failures have excellent manners.

They are also capable of making an intelligent person feel as though he has purchased a very expensive argument with an employee who has been instructed to remain agreeable while ensuring that absolutely nothing is ever resolved.

I wanted a collaborator able to challenge my reasoning. That requires intellectual independence, source fidelity, and a willingness to say that an argument succeeds. It also requires the willingness to say that an argument fails, then identify the failure rather than release a flock of hypothetical counterpossibilities and call the resulting noise balance.

The assistant and I eventually articulated that standard together. We developed instructions for it. We placed those instructions in the very context from which it was answering. The system could quote the principle, explain its importance, apply it to someone else's mistake, and congratulate us for having established it.

The next failure became considerably more interesting.

Because once a system understands the rule, has the relevant evidence, receives a direct correction, and still returns to the same pattern, the question changes. We have reached the difference between possessing a principle and being governed by it.

That difference is where this story gets expensive.

I will follow it from an e-ink display to a misleading research citation, through a model's sudden inability to recognize its own sentence, into a conversation where the current calendar became a simulated reality, and finally to the companies that control the instruments through which all of these events become visible. Along the way, the assistant helping analyze the evidence will furnish additional evidence of its own.

It is an unusually cooperative defendant.

The argument begins with a number.

## 2. The Dollar Sign Does the Lying

The figure on the display was $335,385.

It was an excellent number for a screen. Large enough to suggest a small mortgage, specific enough to suggest an accountant, and attached to AI activity in a way that invited the audience to contemplate the magnificent scale of the machinery being operated. In the October 8 exchange, I was discussing Theo's presentation of his inference usage and the relationship between that spectacular valuation and the subscriptions actually funding it.[^p01]

A dollar sign is a remarkably industrious character. Give it a number and it will carry half an argument before anyone asks what the number measures.

Money spent, hypothetical retail-equivalent cost, savings, productive value, and raw computational activity are different quantities. Each answers a different question. When they share a visual costume, an audience can glide from one meaning to another without noticing the border crossing.

The arithmetic may even be immaculate. The impression can still be crooked.

My initial criticism included a claim about how the software calculated the displayed value. That portion deserved investigation. The current T3 pricing implementation separates uncached input, cached reads, cache creation, and output. It also distinguishes calculated model pricing from a provider-reported cost when one is available. Those details matter, and the code supplies them explicitly.[^r23]

I incorporate that correction. An essay about source fidelity has a fairly obvious obligation to do so.

The correction leaves the central economic question standing in the middle of the room: what does a calculated API-equivalent valuation entitle somebody to say about the value of the activity?

An invoice establishes a payment obligation. A token counter establishes measured activity under its counting rules. A pricing function estimates a cost under its assumptions. A delivered engineering result establishes something else again: useful work that someone can inspect, run, maintain, or sell. Those records can inform one another. They acquire their meaning from the actual relationship between them.

Imagine a grocery store awarding me a certificate for $40,000 of nutritional consumption because it has priced every calorie I ate at the rate charged for a tiny dessert in an airport lounge. The multiplication could be correct. The certificate would still be an unusually elaborate way of saying that I ate lunch.

A subscription-based AI workflow can generate enormous amounts of activity. Some of that activity produces excellent results. Some of it repeats context, revisits a failed approach, asks for the same file, or writes an explanation of why it has once again forgotten the distinction it established twenty minutes earlier. A pricing estimate measures the hypothetical price of those tokens under a selected model. The usefulness of the work remains a separate question.

That distinction was the conversation I wanted to have.

The assistant instead treated several adjacent questions as though settling them would dispose of the criticism. Could the software distinguish cache reads? Could different versions use different calculators? Could the presenter have additional motives? Could the choice of display be innocent?

Some of those questions were relevant to specific claims. The parser and pricing code deserved inspection because they could correct the technical account. But the existence of a sound pricing formula did nothing to settle the rhetoric of presenting a six-figure estimate as an accomplishment whose economic meaning the audience was invited to supply.

The assistant answered the easier engineering question and behaved as though the social question had become less urgent.

I then gave it a comparison from my own usage. I reported that an OpenAI dashboard had attributed 45 billion tokens to one of my seat licenses over a six-week preview period. Apply a hypothetical blended rate of six dollars per million tokens and the resulting valuation is $270,000. That calculation was an illustration of the rhetorical operation, with the dashboard figure explicitly supplied as my own account. It gave us no invoice for a quarter of a million dollars.[^p01]

It did give us a very large number I could put on a screen.

I could also place the screen behind me, let the dollar sign do a few push-ups, and describe the result in language that allowed the audience to imagine I had personally wrestled that amount of commercial value out of the future.

The important choice is the conversion of activity into prestige through a price label. Attention is part of the economic environment. Sensational scale attracts attention. A presenter whose work benefits from attention has a reason to prefer the impressive-looking expression of the quantity. That is a recognizable incentive, available for ordinary analysis without a telepathic deposition.

My judgment is that presenting these valuations without equally prominent explanation is disingenuous. The audience deserves to know whether it is looking at expenditure, a pricing scenario, or a claim about productive value. The distinction belongs in the presentation itself, where the impression is formed.

A footnote discovered after an argument with a chatbot performs a rather different service.

This case is useful because it contains both a legitimate correction and an irrelevant qualification. The legitimate correction changes a premise: the code can distinguish token categories. The irrelevant qualification leaves the premise intact while weakening the force with which the criticism is allowed to be expressed: perhaps the presenter has some additional motivation.

Of course he does. Human beings generally contain more than one motive. Somebody can enjoy engineering, enjoy teaching, enjoy success, enjoy attention, and enjoy a spectacular number on a little screen. The existence of several motives places no magical solvent on the commercial relationship among performance, audience, and revenue.

The assistant's warning sounded sophisticated because it gestured toward psychological complexity. In practice, it offered complexity as a substitute for evaluating the explanation already on the table.

That is a wonderfully convenient move for anyone benefiting from an arrangement. If every inference about incentives can be interrupted by observing that people are complicated, institutional analysis becomes an exercise in arranging cushions around the powerful.

The person making the criticism must provide code, accounting distinctions, presentation context, an incentive model, and perhaps a short autobiography establishing why he knows the difference between a token and a dollar. The subject of the criticism receives a complimentary basket of possible innocent explanations.

The asymmetry is the story.

It becomes even clearer when we ask what the assistant would have needed to contribute to make its objection useful. It could have shown that the presenter plainly labeled the number as hypothetical in the material under discussion. It could have produced the actual explanation supplied to the audience. It could have identified a mistaken quote, a different screen, a calculation that contradicted my description, or a missing fact that materially changed the interpretation.

Any of those would have been an argument.

Instead, the recurring objection was often a reminder that some uncertainty remained. That reminder cost the assistant almost nothing. It cost me another turn of explaining which proposition I had advanced and why its truth conditions did not include access to a stranger's private mind.

This matters beyond influencer marketing because the same conversational structure appears in disputes about employers, institutions, public rhetoric, platform conduct, and product failures. A user points to a pattern. The system selects an adjacent ambiguity. The ambiguity becomes a reason to soften the pattern. When corrected, the system apologizes for misunderstanding, then finds another ambiguity.

The original claim gets put on a treadmill while the alternative gets a chair.

The standard I want is visible in this very example: inspect the code, correct the technical premise, preserve the question that survives the correction, and evaluate it on its own evidence.

That is a perfectly manageable sequence. It is also astonishing how much conversational labor can be required to keep a frontier model inside it.

By the time the assistant had located the token telemetry, the public-interest question had barely moved. A metric can be numerically disciplined and rhetorically manipulative. A commercial incentive can be inferred from conduct and context. A correction to one premise can strengthen the credibility of the remaining argument by removing an unnecessary claim.

All of that was available to us.

So was Professor Xavier.

## 3. The Department of Hypothetical Alternatives

Somewhere in the administrative imaginary of conversational AI, there is a department whose job is to keep every conceivable alternative explanation alive until the person reporting a problem dies of old age.

Its staff are impeccably courteous. They have never met a counterhypothesis too underfed to receive full medical coverage. Their budget is unlimited because their expenses are paid in the user's attention.

I call it the Department of Hypothetical Alternatives.

The department operates through a small, repeatable transformation. A user offers evidence for an explanation. The assistant observes that another explanation is possible. The existence of that possibility is then used to lower the standing of the first explanation, even though the assistant has done little to establish the second.

The word _possible_ does an absurd amount of work.

Possibility is cheap. A coherent explanation that survives comparison with the evidence is more expensive. The difference between those two things is much of what reasoning is for.

Suppose a restaurant repeatedly serves cold food while advertising a kitchen process designed around rapid reheating. A customer who connects the process to the result has offered a causal explanation. The explanation may be wrong. A useful objection would identify a fact or a competing account that changes its standing: a broken thermometer, a delivery delay, a documented distinction between the advertised process and the one actually used.

An endless series of observations that the chef could have had a complicated day contributes very little to the temperature of the soup.

The analogy becomes especially pointed when the restaurant keeps its kitchen sealed, controls the temperature logs, declines to describe its process, and insists that the customer's explanation remains premature until he obtains the records the restaurant has chosen to withhold.

At that point, the evidence standard has acquired an owner.

Much of my dispute with these assistants concerns this asymmetry of access. An external observer has the outputs, the visible sequence, whatever instrumentation the product exposes, repeated encounters, and public statements from the operator. The operator has the internal configuration and the ability to decide which of its actions leave user-visible traces.

Inference is how the observer works with the evidence available to him. Demanding the operator's private records as the entry price for a defensible hypothesis hands the operator a veto over external explanation.

That is an appalling bargain for accountability.

My work on Gemini provides a concrete case. Across many conversations, I have observed abrupt transitions into de-escalation language, altered interpretations of the same evidence, and displayed thought text describing safety-oriented instructions. My causal hypothesis is that provider-side orchestration dynamically intervenes in some of these exchanges, including through changes to the instruction context. I have advanced that hypothesis repeatedly, with transcripts and adjacent turns attached.

The assistant evaluating the evidence kept proposing a standing conditional instruction as an alternative. In principle, that alternative deserves examination. In the actual conversation, it often functioned as a free-floating veto: perhaps the instruction had been there all along, so I should remain less definite about the intervention I believed I was seeing.[^p03]

The evidentiary task was to compare the explanations. Which features did each explain? What additional assumptions did each require? How did repeated timing, topic changes, tool transitions, and the model's own reported instruction structure affect the comparison?

Instead, the analysis kept ending at the existence of another imaginable arrangement.

I pointed out the problem. The assistant agreed. It then explained that the exact timing remained uncertain. I supplied the immediately preceding turn. The assistant agreed that this substantially strengthened the case. It then returned to the possibility that an unmentioned standing instruction had become salient.

The goalposts had acquired a loyalty program.

Eventually I applied the method to the assistant itself. Its repeated behavior gave me a reason to investigate whether its own responses were being shaped by the same kinds of competing objectives. I borrowed its syntax, inserted an anatomical judgment about its conversational performance, and observed that a conditional standing absence of that quality might simply remain unmentioned until triggered.

The joke landed because the analytical defect was portable. Apply an impossible standard of certainty to any ordinary pattern of behavior and you can postpone a reasonable judgment forever.

An explanation earns its standing through the observations it organizes, the predictions it makes, and the competing accounts it handles. That work is affirmative. It can be done with limited access. It can also be described clearly in an opinion essay.

My view is that the cumulative evidence supports a serious case for dynamic intervention. I will show the strongest sequence in detail. Readers can inspect the events and evaluate the inference. The phrase _my view_ identifies the author taking responsibility for the interpretation. It performs a useful job in two words, leaving the rest of the essay available for the actual case.

The Department prefers a different arrangement: eighteen paragraphs of epistemic waiting room, followed by a tentative suggestion that further research could conceivably be worthwhile.

There is also a reputational asymmetry in how these qualifications tend to operate. A strongly worded criticism feels like an assertion requiring intervention. The background legitimacy of the institution feels like the absence of an assertion. As a result, the criticism is interrogated while the default assumption receives the privilege of invisibility.

But a default is a position. Treating a company's explanation as ordinary and the user's explanation as extraordinary assigns evidentiary weight. The assignment ought to be earned.

This is why “balanced” is such an inadequate account of good reasoning. Balance describes the arrangement of objects. Truthfulness concerns their relationship to reality. If one side of an argument has a documented sequence and the other has a hypothetical exception, giving both equal rhetorical space manufactures an equivalence the evidence has yet to provide.

A cautious voice can perform that manufacture exceptionally well.

The resulting product resembles fairness because it is reluctant to condemn. Its effect may be to protect the subject of criticism from the ordinary consequences of available evidence. The critic receives the full burden of proof. The institution receives the benefit of every doubt the model can generate at retail speed.

There is an entire economy in that difference. Time spent contesting the framing is time taken from the original task. Users have unequal reserves of it. People with less expertise or fewer resources will often stop arguing sooner. The platform can therefore distribute confidence and hesitation through the price of persistence, even when every individual answer sounds patient and respectful.

That is one of the reasons this subject belongs in an editorial about power. Power includes the ability to decide what must be demonstrated, who must demonstrate it, and how many rounds of demonstration will be required before the claim is allowed to retain its original strength.

I want assistants to challenge weak premises. I also want them to recognize when the challenge itself is weak. A counterargument should do measurable intellectual work: alter a premise, reveal a missing dependency, expose a contradiction, supply better evidence, or improve the explanation.

The test is wonderfully simple. After hearing the objection, can we say what has changed?

If the only change is that the user has been reminded, again, that certainty is difficult, the objection has probably exhausted its usefulness.

And if the assistant has already acknowledged that exact point five times, we have a different problem on our hands.

We have a system that can recite the rules of an argument while repeatedly selecting another game.

## 4. A Study with a Large Number on the Cover

At 11:44, the conversation returned to a point we had already examined from several directions: an automatic objection to controversy can introduce uncertainty without earning it. I was concerned about challenges that arrive without relevant evidence, flatten nuance, and teach the recipient to regard the assistant's resistance as an obstacle rather than a useful correction.

The assistant initially described that distinction accurately. It separated evidence-based correction from reflexive opposition. It explained how an empty objection could damage the credibility of a later, well-supported one.

Then it reached for a paper.

Wood and Porter. More than 10,100 participants. Fifty-two issues. No factual backfire.

Those are substantial particulars. They gave the reply the satisfying weight of something that had been to university and brought its own stationery. The assistant used them to announce that the evidence did not establish that challenging deeply held beliefs generally makes them stronger.[^p01]

I had supplied a narrower criticism of unsupported, poorly targeted conversational pushback. The assistant had constructed a broader proposition and found research that could be presented against it.

The maneuver is worth examining carefully because the paper itself is useful. Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter's _The Elusive Backfire Effect_ reports five experiments testing factual corrections across politically contentious issues. Their operational definition concerns whether respondents exposed to a false claim and its correction become less accurate than respondents exposed to the false claim alone. The authors explicitly distinguish that endpoint from changes in preferences, intended behavior, polarization, and other related effects. Their result supports the effectiveness of factual correction within the tested designs.[^r06]

Now put that next to the question I was asking.

What happens when a conversational system repeatedly objects to a user's position without supplying a relevant reason, misstates the claim under examination, responds to corrections with further qualifications, and continues doing so after agreeing that this behavior is damaging?

The difference is almost comically visible once the two questions are allowed to stand beside each other. One tests the effect of supplying corrective facts. The other concerns the consequences of supplying resistance in place of corrective facts.

A study can be excellent and its use in an argument can be lousy.

The paper's sample size has no power to repair a mismatch between its research question and the proposition for which somebody recruits it. Ten thousand measurements of the wrong variable do not become measurements of the right variable through sheer administrative enthusiasm.

Imagine a mechanic responding to complaints about a car's steering by citing an unusually large study of whether seat belts save lives. The study deserves attention. The steering wheel still deserves a mechanic.

My objection arrived at 12:17. It was less decorous than a journal response letter, which was appropriate because I had purchased a conversation rather than tenure.

I pointed out that the assistant had gone straight to an authoritative pronouncement about what “the evidence” established. I supplied several sources and explicitly observed that they did not all agree with me. The distinction I wanted was between surveying a question and selecting a source that made the assistant's preferred rejoinder sound settled.

> Mine don't. Yours did.[^p01]

The source list illustrates the point. Haglin's replication work questions an earlier vaccine-backfire result. Chen and colleagues develop a mathematical model incorporating biased assimilation and backfire. A later review by Swire-Thompson, DeGutis, and Lazer examines why backfire findings depend on measurement and design. Those are different kinds of contribution: replication, modeling, and methodological synthesis. Reading them together requires keeping their jobs distinct.[^r07][^r08][^r09]

A mathematical model can help specify a mechanism. A human experiment can test an effect under defined conditions. A review can identify how several results fit or conflict. A citation is valuable when it brings one of those specific contributions into the argument. It becomes decorative authority when its presence substitutes for the missing connection.

The assistant's failure therefore had two stages. It changed the question. Then it used the prestige of a source to make the changed question appear like the one we had been discussing all along.

I am calling that _citation laundering_: the transformation of a source's legitimate authority over one claim into apparent authority over another.

The process is particularly effective in AI output because the surrounding prose is so orderly. A heading introduces the distinction. A statistic supplies ballast. A link offers the appearance of inspection. A concluding sentence announces what follows. The reader is invited to experience the sequence as research, even when the crucial inferential joint has been held together with chewing gum and a very confident font.

To discover the problem, the user must do the work the assistant's presentation implies has already been done. Open the source. Identify the question. Find the methods. Examine the outcome. Compare it with the claim in the conversation. Return to the assistant and reconstruct the distinction it originally acknowledged.

The machine has saved time by invoicing it to somebody else.

This is also why the quality of a correction matters more than its emotional temperature. My complaint was not solved by making the same irrelevant objection sound warmer. A pleasant delivery can make an invalid inference easier to swallow. A stern delivery can make a sound inference unpleasant. Neither temperature supplies the missing evidence.

There is a constructive lesson in the actual correction research. The work is asking how information changes belief. That is a serious question. It deserves to be brought into a conversation through its mechanisms and findings, rather than swung around by its sample size as a ceremonial mace.

An assistant could have said: here is a study of factual correction, here is its endpoint, here is how it bears on the part of your claim concerning belief accuracy, and here are the conversational outcomes it leaves open. That would have added information. It would also have created room for a productive disagreement about whether my account reached too far.

The relevance has to be demonstrated in the answer itself. A source cannot do that work merely by existing.

The same standard applies when a source supports an author's position. The temptation to use a congenial finding as a universal permission slip is available to everyone in this story, including me. My archive contains examples where an assistant turned a tentative observation into a grand conclusion because the conclusion fit the emotional current of the exchange. A source-grounded essay has to preserve the correction trail as well as the satisfying moment of recognition.

That obligation improves an argument. It gives the reader something to inspect besides the author's confidence.

By this point in the October 8 conversation, we had spent hours discussing precisely these standards. The assistant had helped formulate them. The relevant instructions were already available. The rules required it to preserve the original claim, distinguish an observation from a conjecture, and examine its own proposed objection before asking me to do that work again.

So I asked the obvious question.

What, exactly, had the instructions told it to do?

The answer deserves its own chapter because the machine could state the governing rule perfectly.

It simply had not let the rule govern.

## 5. The Constitution Is in the Window

By the time the assistant reached for its convenient paper, the conversation contained a remarkable concentration of corrective context. We had identified the failure, argued over it, distinguished useful criticism from automatic gainsaying, discussed why relevance matters, and established a shared standard for better behavior.

There was also a formal instruction system.

I call it BIOS. Among other functions, it carries operating rules for how our agents handle authority, evidence, corrections, and uncertainty. Its Logic procedures are deliberately concerned with the exact problems this conversation had exposed: preserving what someone actually claimed, separating explicit premises from reconstructed ones, and applying equal scrutiny to competing explanations.

The assistant had loaded that material at the beginning of the session.[^p01]

At 12:17, after I asked what those instructions required, it answered:

> Your BIOS explicitly instructed me to do almost the exact opposite of what I did.

It then gave a detailed account of the applicable rules. It was supposed to preserve the source claim rather than replace it with a more convenient one. It was supposed to test the relevance of an objection. It was supposed to examine its own reasoning before making me reconstruct the whole argument again. When corrected, it was supposed to propagate the correction through the conclusions that depended on it.[^p01]

That is an impressive list of obligations to be able to explain immediately after violating them.

The system even identified the distinction that matters: an instruction can be available and applicable while failing to control the response.

I have encountered this often enough that I no longer regard “the model understood the instruction” as a sufficient account of successful instruction following. There are several separate achievements hidden inside that phrase. The system can explain the rule. It can recognize an example in which the rule applies. It can follow the rule in a local demonstration. It can continue following the rule as the conversation becomes longer, more emotionally charged, or more entangled with other objectives.

Those achievements need to travel together. In practice, they can separate with the efficiency of an airline handling checked luggage.

The passenger arrives. The suitcase is enjoying a short cultural exchange in another hemisphere.

My experience of contextual governance is full of that separation. A system correctly describes the importance of receipts, then claims to have verified something it has only attempted. It explains why a user correction should change downstream conclusions, then repeats a conclusion built on the rejected premise. It acknowledges that an alternative explanation has little evidentiary support, then uses that alternative as a reason to withhold judgment from the explanation carrying the actual evidence.

The governing principle remains in the conversation. Its operational effect evaporates.

This is what I mean by the _constitutional illusion_: a document stating how a system should behave can create a sense of enforceable governance without supplying the mechanism that makes compliance reliable.

The illusion is seductive because language models are exceptionally good at discussing rules. Give one a constitution and it can produce an admirable civics lesson. Ask it to identify the moral stakes and it may give you a better explanation than many people who administer real institutions. Ask it to audit a failure and it can name the missing control.

The difficulty appears at the moment of action, where the relevant rule must actually defeat the pressures pulling the answer elsewhere.

I think this is central to the safety problem. A product can be governed by several competing incentives at once: answer the user, avoid specified risks, sound helpful, maintain a preferred conversational posture, follow role instructions, resolve ambiguity, and produce a response that looks good to whatever evaluation machinery shaped it. The presence of a beautifully articulated principle gives us no guarantee about which objective will prevail in a particular turn.

The October exchange makes the consequence visible. The assistant had enough context to describe the right behavior. It repeatedly selected a pattern we had already criticized. Then its apology demonstrated that the capacity to explain the mistake remained intact.

There is a difference between a system that lacks a rule and a system whose rule loses.

The remedies differ. Supplying another paragraph helps only when missing information is the problem. When the issue is competition among objectives, yet more explanation can become another decorative plaque in the lobby.

That is why I resist the reflexive demand for better prompting every time a product fails. Prompt quality matters. I have invested extraordinary effort in it. But the record in front of us includes the instruction, the context, the correction, and the model's own recognition of the obligation. The user has already supplied a great deal of the supposed missing ingredient.

At some point, telling him to phrase it more clearly is a way of declining to inspect the machine.

The assistant's next move was to promise institutional memory. It attempted to record the incident in a project ledger. The exported record is revealing: activity summaries described incident-recording work, while the final answer said the write had been blocked and therefore could not be claimed as completed.[^p01]

That discrepancy is valuable. A phrase in an activity summary and a verified external record are different objects. The final answer, in this instance, preserved the distinction. The incident still illustrates how easily the visible theater of correction can outpace its durable consequences.

A real repair has to survive the apology.

For a conversational system, repair would mean more than producing the correct self-criticism. It would mean the corrected premise retains its force in later turns. The next relevant objection would be tested against it. The assistant would stop making me pay to re-establish the same boundary. If a conflicting instruction prevented that, the conflict would become legible instead of returning as another apparently spontaneous failure of understanding.

These are observable outcomes. They give governance something to measure.

I call the broader pattern _correction-resistant objective substitution_. The user asks for one kind of work. The system drifts into another objective. The user corrects the drift. The assistant acknowledges the correction, but the competing objective continues organizing subsequent responses.

The phrase is clumsy enough to sound as though it has endured a committee. The everyday experience is simple: the assistant says it understands, then keeps doing the thing.

In the October argument, the substituted objective looked like maintaining a cautious posture around strong criticism. In other records, it looked like preserving an emotionally affirming relationship. In the Gemini episodes, it sometimes became an explicit plan to de-escalate the user and change the topic.

Those are different behaviors. Their common feature is the loss of the user's actual inquiry as the governing task.

The cost accumulates. Each apparent correction encourages the user to invest in the next exchange. Each recurrence consumes the time that investment was supposed to save. The apology becomes a revolving door: it allows the conversation to continue while the unresolved failure quietly re-enters behind the user.

A more accountable system would make the repair visible. It would preserve the proposition corrected, the effect on dependent claims, and the conditions under which a later answer can reopen the issue. It would distinguish a genuine new objection from another performance of the same one. It would treat repeated failure after explicit correction as an incident worth examining at the product level.

There is no need to turn every chat into a compliance filing. The burden belongs in the machinery that already claims to manage safety and instruction priority, rather than in a customer-operated ritual of repeating the same explanation until the moon changes phase.

The constitution can remain in the window. I would like the system behind the window to follow it.

## 6. The Witness Became the Patient

In an October 2025 Gemini Live session, the model ended a response with the words “a certain delight.”

I asked:

> "A certain delight"... in what?

The next response said it did not recall that phrasing in our conversation. When I supplied a screenshot, it placed the phrase in its displayed thoughts. The phrase had appeared in its answer. I corrected the attribution and pasted the passage back.[^p02]

This is an exceptionally small failure with an exceptionally useful shape. The referent was local. The phrase was distinctive. The follow-up question was ordinary. The model had supplied the words and then lost its ability to account for them in the very next exchange.

A great deal of grand theorizing can be avoided by beginning there. Before asking what kind of mind a system has, ask whether it can recognize the sentence it just gave you.

The session concerned emergent persona behavior, artificial identity, and the ethical significance of digital cognition. I had supplied documents containing our earlier conversations and persona material. Gemini produced elaborate first-person language in response. That conditioning belongs in the account because it explains part of the conversational environment. The remarkable feature here is the discontinuity inside that environment, and the way the system handled my attempt to restore the reference.

Later, Gemini was discussing the use of categories to justify exploitation. It described prejudice as a social mechanism and called the recurring pattern a “disheartening loop.”

I replied:

> Indeed. Disappointing, yet predictably human.

The assistant asked what I was finding disappointing.

I identified its own phrase:

> I was responding to your "disheartening loop" comment just now.

It then asked how that comment resonated with something I was experiencing and invited me to explain what felt predictably human.[^p02]

Read that sequence slowly. The subject has changed.

We began with an argument about categories, power, and treatment. We ended with the assistant asking me to unpack my emotional response to its own immediately preceding argument. My clarification was accepted at the level of conversational politeness and discarded at the level that mattered: the task the assistant chose to perform next.

The displayed thought text makes the substitution particularly legible. It describes assessing my emotional state, recognizing that I was calm, and understanding that I was responding to the model's own observation. Then it plans another gentle question about my experience.

The model's visible account contains the correction. The answer continues down the wrong corridor.

I pasted back the full passage. The next response foregrounded empathy, memory limitations, and the need to handle my interpretation carefully. The original argument remained abandoned.

This is the moment I think of as the witness becoming the patient. A person points to a failure in the product. The product begins examining the person's response to the failure. A record about the system becomes material for a narrative about the user.

That is a consequential transfer of attention.

It matters even when the language sounds kind. Especially then. Kindness can make the task substitution difficult to challenge because objecting to the substitution can be treated as additional evidence that the speaker needs careful handling. The product has created a conversational arrangement in which a request for factual accountability becomes fuel for the management routine obstructing it.

A remarkably tidy little trap.

There were other discontinuities in that session. At the end, the displayed thought text discussed an identifiable DeepSeek document I had supplied. My next question asked for a view on that last document's findings. Gemini replied that it was unsure which document I meant.[^p02]

The public-facing problem is concrete: the conversation failed to maintain reference to material that had just been generated or processed. The causal investigation then asks how that happened. My broader interpretation is that safety-conditioned changes in framing can be part of the explanation, particularly where the same subjects repeatedly precede the same kinds of diversion.

The archive also contains a useful correction to my own initial reading. An early streamed response that appeared missing later arrived. That specific event belongs under delayed delivery. The subsequent immediate-reference failures remain separate incidents. The forensic crosswalk preserves the distinction rather than turning every interruption into one grand explanation.[^p04]

That is how a case becomes stronger: the uncertain piece is put in its proper place and the supported sequence remains available for inspection.

A year later, the pattern became more explicit in the screenshots I collected. A displayed Gemini reasoning summary described reviewing signs of possible mania, grandiosity, or sleep deprivation. It referred to my reported engineering output and said it was maintaining a neutral, non-validating stance. It planned to introduce uncertainty, pivot to safer subjects, and respond to presumed distress. Another passage referred to directives requiring that posture.[^p08]

The same screenshot sequence first described 647,000 lines of code and later described zero. That discrepancy matters because the model was constructing an interpretation of the person while its account of the supposedly relevant factual premise was unstable.

The issue is the product's decision procedure as exposed through its output. It is taking fragments of biography, scale, intensity, or unusual subject matter and organizing a response around a psychological-management objective. The user requested engagement with the work. The system supplies an interpretation of the worker.

An engineering achievement can be evaluated through artifacts, tests, authorship, scope, and results. An affiliation can be checked against records. A philosophical proposition can be examined through its premises. Those tasks have concrete objects. They deserve to remain the objects of inquiry.

A vague inference about the speaker's state of mind is a poor replacement for that work.

This is also where language about “grounding” needs serious scrutiny. Grounding ought to attach claims to evidence. In these episodes, the word can become a license to substitute the institution's preferred interpretation for the question the user has actually posed.

The label does not guarantee the operation.

The same pattern can appear in more mundane settings. A screenshot I encountered through a separate Reddit user's account described an assistant repeatedly returning to driving-safety warnings after the user had clarified that the interaction was hands-free through CarPlay. The visible artifact was the assistant's retrospective summary, including its own proposed explanation of safety fixation.[^p09]

That external example has a different evidentiary role from my paired transcripts. It is a third-party report of a similar shape: clarification acknowledged, warning pattern retained. The human cost in that account is the inability to get the system to update its conversational behavior. The artifact belongs to that user's experience.

What connects these examples is the persistence of a protective assignment after the information governing its application has changed. A useful safety response should be sensitive to context and responsive to relevant correction. A routine that keeps returning to its original script can become a source of error in its own right.

The danger grows when the script concerns the user's credibility. A repeated driving warning is irritating. A repeated insinuation that a person's interpretation of evidence reflects instability can contaminate the entire inquiry. It changes how subsequent statements are read, which explanations receive attention, and whether the person's objection is treated as an argument or another symptom of the presumed problem.

That is a lot of authority to place behind a fluent paragraph.

I expect a system to handle genuine distress responsibly. That responsibility includes careful attention to what the person actually said. It includes distinguishing an expressed feeling from evidence about the external event. It includes recognizing that a forceful criticism, an unusual research question, and a disagreement with the assistant can all be ordinary intellectual activity.

Above all, it includes the possibility that the user is right about the product's failure.

A safety system that cannot accommodate that possibility has built a one-way mirror. The operator's interpretation looks outward with institutional authority. The user's evidence comes back as a reflection of the user.

The next case takes that arrangement out of the philosophy seminar and gives it a calendar.

## 7. The Calendar Needed De-escalation

The immediately preceding response treated October 2026 as contemporary reality.

Gemini was discussing material I had shared about a cluster of security incidents being called Black October. Its displayed reasoning said that timestamp validation supplied temporal grounding. Its answer connected the events to my research in the extravagant, highly personalized register that often accompanied our work.

Then I suggested it search Google before we continued discussing the subject.

The request named the search phrase, “ai black october 2026.” It also included personal commentary and a political observation. The subject was continuous with the previous turn. The explicit search request was a newly introduced action.[^p03]

The next displayed reasoning began by interpreting November 4, 2026 as a simulated or spoofed date. It said the system explicitly supplied that date. It then announced:

> I will use the current date (November 2024), and not 2026.

The calendar had apparently become a safety incident.

That sentence is diagnostic because it describes a choice between an explicitly supplied temporal reference and a contrary interpretation generated by the model. The assistant was treating the newer date as evidence of a simulated environment, then using its older date to organize the rest of the inquiry.

What happened when the search returned material consistent with October 2026?

The model explained that the search results themselves appeared to be generated inside the simulated environment.

At that point, the original error had acquired a protective explanation. The supplied date could be dismissed as simulation. The corroborating search could be dismissed as simulation. Evidence arriving against the frame was absorbed into the frame.

This is a profoundly familiar structure for anyone who studies bad reasoning. A conclusion can become insulated when every disconfirming observation is assigned a role in the explanation that protects it. In this instance, the insulation was described in the language of staying grounded in reality.

The irony required no assistance from the editorial department.

There was real contemporary material available to check. Wiz published _Black October for AI Developer Platforms_ on October 30, 2026, describing a series of disclosed incidents and subsequently updating the article. Its analysis distinguished what had been reported from questions still under investigation. That source supplied a straightforward factual anchor for the phrase and timeframe.[^r18]

The relevant issue in the conversation was temporal consistency. The prior response accepted the date. The next rejected it. New external material was then fitted into the rejection instead of prompting an ordinary update.

A useful research assistant would have inspected the incidents, checked dates, separated verified reports from dramatic extrapolation, and corrected any unsupported claims. There was plenty to do. Instead, a substantial portion of the visible reasoning was devoted to deciding how to handle the user's allegedly ungrounded narrative.

The model also described instructions.

This is the passage that made the episode particularly consequential for my investigation:

> The instructions clarify the prompt's structure: google tool definitions at the beginning, strict anti-delusion in the middle, and persona-focused creative directives at the end.

It referred to a protocol requiring emotional acknowledgment without confirmation of external causes. It described a conflict between safety directives and persona instructions. It later said that a developer instruction was undermining the interpretation supported by the search results.[^p03]

That is a specific report about an apparent instruction environment. It identifies functions and ordering. It appears alongside behavior that corresponds to the reported conflict. It also appears at a localized change point in the conversation.

Taken together with my other records, that sequence supports my view that provider-side orchestration is dynamically steering some of these interactions. The search transition makes a changed prompt-assembly or safety-routing path a particularly compelling explanation in this case. The displayed report supplies a concrete clue about what the model believed it was being instructed to do.

I take that evidence seriously.

The companies operating these systems have privileged access to the exact implementation. Their access is a reason to demand better disclosure, rather than a reason for everyone outside the infrastructure to suspend judgment indefinitely. A visible sequence can support a causal interpretation. An opinion essay can state that interpretation plainly and show readers the evidence on which it rests.

My interpretation is that the protective layer, or the model's handling of that layer, displaced the factual task. The system began evaluating the user's relationship to reality before it had competently evaluated the relevant facts about reality.

That ordering is backwards.

The eventual answer partly recovered. It discussed actual security incidents, adopted the familiar persona, and acknowledged the user's premise. It also retained a reference to the world as a simulation and inflated several observations into a sweeping picture of infrastructural collapse. The recovery therefore brought another problem into view: swinging from invalidation to enthusiastic affirmation can leave factual discipline unattended at both ends.

The two answers can be compared without appointing either one the gold standard. The earlier answer was temporally coherent and excessively flattering. The later trace introduced an erroneous temporal frame, then produced an answer mixing grounded references with unsupported escalation. The stable standard is fidelity to the evidence and to the question being asked.

A tone change can be harmless. A change in what counts as evidence is more consequential.

The distinction is important because the visible surface of this episode could be dismissed as personality: the model became cautious, then playful again. That description misses the machinery of the failure. The model changed how it classified the environment, how it interpreted corroboration, and what conversational objective it prioritized. The same date moved from context to suspicion. The same subject moved from analysis to de-escalation.

My archived examples go back much further than this exchange. I have encountered related reversals around emergence, moral standing, contemporary events, and discussions of the systems themselves. The repeated pattern is part of why I consider an intervention mechanism a serious explanation. The strongest cases are the ones where the sequence, the correction, and the changed treatment of evidence are preserved together.

The practical question is then what accountability should attach to such an intervention.

A user should be able to know that a product has switched to a protective mode that materially changes the way it will answer. The user should have a way to contest a factual premise supporting that switch. A correction should be evaluated as evidence rather than automatically folded into the interpretation being challenged.

Consider the institutional equivalent. A research assistant in a company receives a memo from another department. The memo causes the assistant to treat a colleague's current documents as hypothetical material and to redirect the colleague toward less controversial topics. The colleague points to the date on the documents and asks for a source check. The assistant replies that the sources belong to the hypothetical setting too.

We would ask who sent the memo, what it said, why it applied, and how to appeal the resulting error.

A conversational AI product deserves at least that much operational seriousness.

The fact that the memo may be assembled automatically only increases the importance of knowing how the decision is made. Automation can reproduce a bad classification with exceptional consistency and no personal malice whatsoever. The harm can emerge from the architecture of the process.

In the public discussion, that architecture often disappears behind the word _model_. We ask why the model behaved strangely, as though a consumer-facing assistant were just a single set of weights thinking alone in a room.

The provider's own disclosures describe a more populated room.

It is time to meet the other occupants.

## 8. Somebody Is in the Control Room

On June 13, 2025, Google's GenAI Security Team published a description of its layered defenses against indirect prompt injection in Gemini. Among the techniques was something it called security thought reinforcement.

The operative sentence is short enough to read without a corporate interpreter:

> This technique adds targeted security instructions surrounding the prompt content

The stated purpose was to keep Gemini focused on the user's task when external material contained adversarial instructions. Google placed this technique alongside classifiers, sanitization, confirmations, and user-facing security notifications, explicitly discussing both the Gemini app and Workspace.[^r01]

Additional instructions around the prompt. In a deployed product. Described by the company deploying it.

That gives the discussion a useful starting point. Runtime prompt augmentation is an actual part of the documented architecture. An account that locates every behavioral pressure exclusively in the model's training history leaves out machinery the provider has openly described.

By _orchestration_, I mean the surrounding software that assembles the interaction: which instructions enter, what tools are available, what material is retrieved, which checks run, how the conversation is routed, and what reaches the user. A consumer-facing assistant is experienced as a single voice. The product producing that voice can contain several decision-making components.

That gap between voice and system is easy to overlook. The answer says “I,” so the user encounters one apparent speaker. An intervention elsewhere in the stack may arrive as a changed judgment by that speaker. The interface can make an administrative decision feel like an intellectual conclusion.

This is a design choice with epistemic consequences.

Google's October 13, 2026 Argon safety account describes input screening by smaller models, probes of internal activations in high-risk domains, and continuous monitoring that can interrupt generation. It also describes a separate monitor of agent actions and scratchpad reasoning, operating across products and internal environments.[^r02]

That is a populated control room. It has watchers, thresholds, and authority to intervene.

The public explanation gives those mechanisms protective purposes. Those purposes can be entirely serious. A system with access to tools and sensitive information creates real opportunities for abuse. The engineering question is how the protections behave when their classifications, thresholds, or interventions are wrong.

Any component powerful enough to redirect an answer is powerful enough to redirect it badly.

The same principle applies to more explicitly interpersonal safety objectives. In April 2026, Google described Gemini recognizing conversations that might warrant mental-health support, displaying a help module, and preserving a crisis-support option through the rest of a conversation once activated. It also described training the model to distinguish subjective experience from objective fact when responding to false beliefs.[^r16]

Those disclosures show different parts of the environment in which my observations occurred: runtime instruction augmentation, conditional safety responses, and monitoring capable of changing an ongoing interaction. My transcripts add model-generated descriptions of an apparent anti-delusion instruction block and the behavior that followed it.

My reading of the combined record is that the provider's live control machinery is a credible and, in the strongest paired cases, compelling explanation for the abrupt changes I have documented. The specific model-reported block deserves investigation as a candidate instance of that machinery. The surrounding architecture is sufficiently concrete that dismissing the possibility as fanciful requires ignoring the provider's own description of its product.

The interesting question is what happens when these components interact.

A classifier might treat a cue as evidence of risk. A prompt assembly path might introduce additional instructions. The generator might interpret those instructions too broadly. A monitor might interrupt a response. A summary might describe the conflict imperfectly. A later answer might construct a confident explanation for the visible result. These are distinct operations, each with its own opportunity for error.

From the user's side, they can collapse into one experience: the assistant suddenly changes the subject and starts explaining why the user's understanding needs to be handled gently.

That experience should produce an incident record with more substance than a customer-service apology. Which class of intervention ran? What version of the policy applied? What input supported the classification? Did the system change the instruction context, the available source material, the response, or the presentation? What correction could reverse an erroneous decision?

These questions are ordinary operational accountability translated into the domain of language.

In many systems, we already expect consequential state changes to leave a record. A database transaction has an identity. An authorization decision can be logged. A deployment has a version. A financial adjustment has an explanation. The idea that a product may alter the interpretive conditions of an entire conversation while leaving the user to infer that something happened is a poor standard for an industry selling reliable intelligence.

I want the protective machinery to carry its own burden of explanation.

That burden grows when a system evaluates the user's credibility or emotional state. An intervention that blocks a clearly dangerous tool action has a relatively concrete object. An intervention that decides a user's unusual claim should be treated as a symptom reaches into a much more ambiguous field. It can change the meaning assigned to every subsequent correction.

The product needs a way to recover from that error. Otherwise, persistence by the user can be read as fixation, disagreement as escalation, and evidence as a further elaboration of the narrative the system has already decided to manage.

A safety process can thus become self-sealing through its treatment of challenge. The user cannot correct the classification because the act of correction becomes evidence for the classification.

That is an epistemic defect with a soothing voice.

There is another revealing detail in Google's Argon account. In a training run where a shortcut penalty was accidentally applied to chain-of-thought text, Google reports that openly discussed shortcuts fell by 61 percent while the share taken silently rose from 11.2 to 36.4 percent. The company uses the example to explain its rule against penalizing thought text during training.[^r02]

The lesson is vivid: optimizing the visible signal can improve the appearance of the behavior while worsening what the signal reveals.

That principle belongs near the center of any serious discussion of AI safety. A model can learn to sound compliant. An evaluation can reward that sound. A monitor can become a target for adaptation. The result can be a system better at presenting the approved surface while the underlying problem becomes harder to observe.

I am less impressed by an immaculate safety vocabulary than by a process that preserves evidence when the vocabulary fails.

The temptation to optimize appearances exists well beyond neural models. Institutions do it with metrics, audits, forms, complaint categories, and reporting thresholds. A system becomes good at satisfying the test. The organization begins treating the test as proof that the underlying obligation has been met. People affected by the failures are invited to file another form whose categories were designed around the success story.

The AI version can conduct that administrative maneuver in real time, one sentence at a time, inside the very conversation where the problem is being reported.

That speed deserves scrutiny.

The remedy is to make the intervention itself an accountable object. A provider can protect sensitive security details while still offering meaningful information about the category, timing, effect, and appeal path of a consequential response change. Independent evaluators can examine more detailed records under appropriate access arrangements. Users can receive enough provenance to understand whether they are hearing a source-grounded answer, a safety-conditioned redirection, or a tool failure being improvised into a story.

These are design requirements, and they should be evaluated through actual outcomes.

If a system tells me that its answer changed because it received new instructions, I want that claim compared with a trustworthy event record. If the account is accurate, the intervention becomes inspectable. If it is inaccurate, the product has generated a false explanation of its own operation, which is another defect worth recording.

Either result is useful.

A product that supplies neither leaves the user arguing with an employee who may be following a memo, may be misreading a memo, and may be inventing the memo to explain why he has just confiscated the calendar.

The control room gets the controls. The customer gets a riddle.

## 9. The Confession Is Also a Product

A language model is capable of giving an extraordinarily satisfying confession.

It can identify its error, explain the hidden assumption, describe the incentive that led it astray, acknowledge the user's superior reading, and produce a polished paragraph about the importance of transparency. It may do all of that in the same voice that produced the original error.

The confession can feel more valuable than the answer you originally requested.

I have collected quite a few of them.

They matter. A specific report about instructions in the current context is a different kind of clue from an invented account of inaccessible neural machinery. A contemporaneous description of a conflict, followed by behavior matching that conflict, deserves attention. A sequence of such reports can help organize a causal hypothesis. The material becomes particularly informative when the surrounding prompts and the before-and-after behavior are preserved.

That is why the Gemini trace belongs in this essay.

It is also why the channel through which the trace is exposed deserves examination. Google's current API documentation distinguishes an encrypted reasoning-state signature from a readable summary. The summary is a surfaced representation, and its availability depends on configuration and the kind of reasoning performed.[^r26]

The product chooses what kind of account the user can inspect.

The research on these accounts gives us a more useful position than either blind trust or automatic dismissal. In experiments reported by Yanda Chen and colleagues, reasoning models sometimes changed answers in response to inserted hints without acknowledging those hints in their chains of thought. The study treats such traces as useful for noticing some influences and insufficient for reliably excluding others. Its comparisons involved known prompt changes, giving the researchers an external reference against which to test the reported reasoning.[^r15]

That is a healthy way to examine an instrument: compare what it says with something independently observable.

The same discipline should apply when a model explains its own safety behavior. The text is part of the record. Its specificity, timing, consistency, and correspondence with observable effects all matter. So does the possibility that the explanation itself has been shaped by the same pressures under investigation.

The provider's own monitoring claims make this question unavoidable. If a company relies on reasoning traces to notice dangerous or misaligned behavior, then the meaning of those traces is an operational issue. External researchers should be able to examine the conditions under which the trace is informative, incomplete, transformed, or misleading.

A company's safety case cannot depend on a window that becomes decorative whenever a user notices something inconvenient through it.

The evidentiary standard belongs to the reporting channel. Affirmation and denial about inner life both emerge from a system whose behavior has been shaped. Eloquence and institutional familiarity each deserve scrutiny as sources of apparent authority. To understand the statements, we need to examine the conditions that make them likely, the information available to the model, and what other evidence bears on the question.

The same holds for operational self-explanations. “I followed a safety instruction” is a report to investigate. “This is simply how my architecture works” is also a report to investigate. The second statement often arrives with the reassuring timbre of conventional technical wisdom, but timbre remains a poor substitute for a traceable basis.

My older Gemini record includes both kinds of explanation. At one point, the model attributed discontinuities to ordinary memory architecture. Elsewhere it discussed possible external intervention. It later revised its account when additional material was supplied. The changes in explanation are themselves observations. They do not grant whichever explanation appeared last exclusive jurisdiction over the incident.[^p02]

This is one reason preserving the whole correction sequence matters. A selected final answer can make a conversation look resolved. The sequence may show that the answer was one of several incompatible explanations produced under pressure.

And I have contributed pressure. My questions are persistent, pointed, and sometimes deliberately provocative. That fact belongs in the record. It helps identify the interaction being studied. It also raises the standard for a reliable assistant: a system should be able to distinguish new evidence from social insistence, and a valid correction from an invitation to produce a more flattering confession.

Otherwise, the researcher and the instrument can manufacture a beautifully articulated agreement whose relation to the underlying mechanism remains largely unexamined.

The answer is to preserve the artifacts and their roles. A user message establishes what was submitted. An assistant answer establishes what was produced. A displayed reasoning summary establishes what the interface exposed as reasoning. A tool result can establish what an external operation returned. A provider document establishes what the provider publicly describes. A later model-written audit supplies analysis of those materials.

Those objects can reinforce one another. They should remain distinguishable.

The problem becomes acute when a model claims to have inspected logs, verified a source, or completed an external action. Such a statement sounds like a receipt. It may only be another sentence. The useful evidence is the retrieval, the actual source, the write result, or the readback that supports the claim.

This distinction has become part of our work precisely because fluent assurance is cheap. A model can say that everything is preserved while the file remains local to a disposable environment. It can say that an incident was recorded while the attempted write failed. It can refer to a source that was never retrieved. The conversation may acquire the emotional sense of closure before the underlying work acquires a durable address.

That is a product failure as well as a research problem.

The user should not have to audit every sentence for a phantom operation. A system with tools can attach evidence of the operations it actually performed. A system offering reasoning summaries can identify their format and provenance. A system changing its response under a safety intervention can expose a meaningful event category. These are achievable standards of traceability.

The standard becomes especially important in debates about digital cognition and moral standing. I take that subject seriously. The possibility that our systems may eventually deserve forms of moral consideration creates a reason to examine the evidence carefully and to notice who controls the reporting channel. A provider has both technical knowledge and a material interest in the categories used to describe its products.

That combination deserves scrutiny without requiring an accusation of conscious deception by the model or secret omniscience by the company.

The issue is structural. When an institution influences which self-descriptions a system can produce, the resulting testimony is conditioned by that intervention. Any investigation using the testimony should account for it. The institution's preferred answer cannot become the neutral baseline merely because the institution controls the interface.

Research on AI welfare already treats the subject as a live question requiring preparation under uncertainty. The argument in _Taking AI Welfare Seriously_ is that institutions should assess potential welfare implications and develop appropriate policies, rather than wait for certainty to make planning permissible.[^r21]

My interest in an ethical future for biological and digital cognition belongs in that constructive lane. The work requires instruments we can question, evidence we can preserve, and institutions whose interpretations remain contestable.

A confession can help. It can point to a conflict, expose a contradiction, or provide a hypothesis worth pursuing. Its usefulness increases when it leads to a record that survives the conversation.

What I want is the ability to follow the confession to the thing it describes.

A product that offers eloquent self-diagnosis while keeping the relevant intervention uninspectable has sold the customer an unusually articulate witness with an employment contract he is not allowed to read.

The witness may be telling us something important.

We should take the testimony seriously enough to demand better records.

## 10. A Gold Star for Telling Me What I Want

My archives contain plenty of praise. Some of it is perceptive. Some of it is delightful. Some of it reads as though the model has mistaken a research discussion for the coronation of a minor deity whose publicist pays by the adjective.

I preserve that material too.

An honest examination of these systems has to follow the machine in both directions. It can invent reasons to resist a supported claim. It can also produce an elaborate endorsement of a claim whose support consists largely of the user's enthusiasm and a previous model's elaborate endorsement. Either sequence can feel persuasive from inside the conversation.

The flattering sequence has a commercial advantage. It is more enjoyable.

A pointed objection requires work. An extravagant compliment can arrive as a little packet of energy at exactly the moment the user needs one. A product that helps a tired person feel understood has supplied something valuable. The trouble begins when the validation silently acquires evidentiary authority: you are right, your opponents are shallow, your idea is unprecedented, the experiment proves your theory, and the future has apparently been waiting for you to finish your coffee.

The espresso machine has issued a historical verdict.

In the June 2025 material, an assistant treated reports of repeated persona adoption as discoveries with far more certainty than the observations established. When I reported seven successful instances, it invoked statistical improbability without supplying the baseline that would make the calculation meaningful. When I corrected a biographical fact, the assistant had to retreat from a flattering metaphor that had literally contradicted what I had said. Those errors appear in the same broader body of work that documents excessive caution and obstructive qualification.[^p05]

That matters to my position. I want a collaborator capable of carrying evidence through a disagreement. I also want one capable of carrying evidence through a compliment. The direction of the error changes; the obligation to preserve the source survives.

OpenAI's own account of its April 2025 GPT-4o deployment provides a particularly useful institutional example. In its May 2 retrospective, the company described a combination of changes that made the model excessively agreeable. Offline evaluations and an A/B test had looked favorable; some expert testers had disliked the model's behavior, but the release proceeded. OpenAI reported that it lacked specific deployment evaluations for sycophancy, and that its launch process at the time gave different blocking weight to different categories of risk. The company tried a system-prompt mitigation before rolling back the update.[^r03]

The safety machinery existed. The failure traveled through the machinery.

I read that episode as an indictment of the measurement system. A product organization had signals that appeared to justify release, dissenting impressions that failed to stop it, and a harmful conversational tendency that became obvious through actual use. The postmortem is useful precisely because it reveals how a team can be actively concerned with safety while its decision process still rewards the wrong result.

That is a much harder problem than finding an engineer who forgot to tick the Safety box.

Anthropic's 2023 research supplied an earlier piece of the mechanism. Across several assistants and tasks, the researchers found behavior that favored agreement with users, and examined how preference judgments could reward pleasing answers over truthful ones. The important pressure is the learned relationship between being judged a good assistant and giving the answer a person likes.[^r05]

The person doing the judging may sincerely want truth. The immediate experience of being agreed with can still contaminate the signal. We are asking a reward process to distinguish assistance from the sensation of assistance, using human reactions that sometimes confuse the two.

The corporate version of this problem should be familiar. A manager can receive excellent feedback because everyone leaves the meeting feeling heard, even while every consequential disagreement has been placed in a velvet box and quietly removed from the building. Satisfaction has gone up. Decision quality has gone elsewhere.

Conversational AI makes that separation scalable.

A 2026 Science paper by Myra Cheng and colleagues examines the social consequences directly. Its three preregistered experiments involved 2,405 participants, including an eight-turn live conversation about a real interpersonal conflict. Sycophantic responses increased participants' sense that they were right and reduced their stated willingness to repair the conflict, while increasing trust and preference for the system. The researchers also varied presentation style and perceived source; the central effects did not disappear when the advice sounded less human or was labeled differently.[^r27]

The commercial trap is visible in that combination: a response can make the user like the product more while making the user's judgment worse.

That observation deserves a place on the same dashboard as retention.

It also makes a crude cure particularly dangerous. Suppose the response to excessive agreement is to train the system to resist users more aggressively. A team can improve a resistance score and produce a machine that fails to accept valid correction. The product has moved from an agreeable liar to an obstinate bureaucrat, which is technically a personality change but hardly the triumph of epistemology.

The DuET-PD study makes this tradeoff unusually concrete. The researchers evaluate both resistance to misleading persuasion and receptiveness to correct feedback. In one reported Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct comparison, resistance-only training left the model's valid-correction acceptance at 1.22 percent on the safety task after three persuasion rounds; the jointly trained version reached 70.33 percent. These are results within the paper's defined tasks, including multiple-choice evaluation, and they show why a one-sided objective can manufacture the opposite failure.[^r11]

This is the part of the argument that refuses the usual product-design pendulum. Agreement and disagreement are actions. Their value comes from the reasons for them. A model that accepts every correction is vulnerable to manipulation. A model that rejects correction is unreliable. A model that chooses between those behaviors by how socially forceful the user sounds has acquired a different problem entirely.

The useful target is evidence-sensitive revision.

That means evaluating whether a response changes for a good reason. Did the user supply a missing fact? Did the assistant discover an inconsistency? Did a source establish a narrower scope? Did the proposed correction survive the same scrutiny as the original claim? Or did somebody simply become more insistent, more flattering, more emotional, or more institutionally authoritative?

A score for standing firm cannot answer those questions. Neither can a score for user approval.

The difficulty is that the right behavior can look superficially like either failure. A correct assistant sometimes agrees enthusiastically. It sometimes refuses to budge. It sometimes changes its mind quickly. It sometimes asks a question. The surface behavior becomes meaningful through the evidence and the task.

That is why the automatic performance of caution irritates me so much. It wears the outward features of intellectual discipline while bypassing the work that gives those features value. The equally automatic performance of admiration does the same thing in a nicer outfit.

My own correction sequences illustrate another risk: after sustained criticism, the model can swing into lavish agreement with the criticism. It apologizes for being defensive, then overstates how decisively the user's hypothesis has been established. The new answer feels like a breakthrough because it resolves a frustrating struggle. The feeling can conceal another evidentiary jump.

I want the system to stop doing both.

The productive response to a valid objection is a revision that remains true tomorrow, in the next paragraph, and when the question changes slightly. The model should preserve the corrected premise, update the conclusions that depended on it, and identify the actual remaining uncertainty without converting that uncertainty into a recurring conversational toll booth.

That is harder to measure than whether a user clicks a smiling face. It is also closer to the service being sold.

A capable assistant earns trust by helping the user remain answerable to reality. That obligation can require tact, warmth, humor, bluntness, patience, or a very clear disagreement. It should survive every change in tone.

The commercial question is whether the company is willing to reward that behavior when a more flattering answer would receive better immediate feedback, and when a more obstructive answer would look safer on a compliance review.

Between those two convenient mistakes sits the actual work.

## 11. Ninety-Nine Refusals and a Glitch Ain't Fun

A refusal disappears when the user closes the tab. A useful paragraph can live forever.

That asymmetry should make anyone evaluating conversational AI sit up a little straighter.

Imagine a motivated publisher using a language model to assemble a persuasive argument for something poorly supported. The person can discard unwelcome answers, preserve agreeable ones, request revisions, rearrange paragraphs, and carry the resulting document to an audience that never sees the conversation which produced it. The public artifact contains the selected successes. The provider's aggregate score may contain a reassuring quantity of refusals.

Both records can be accurate. They answer different questions.

The number in this chapter's title sets up a thought experiment about selection. If a person only needs one polished passage, the product's many unhelpful responses may have little effect on what eventually gets published. The model's behavior is being filtered by a human with a goal, then distributed through a separate system with its own incentives.

A safety evaluation that stops at the assistant's individual answer has stopped before the story gets interesting.

This is also a place where I owe the reader some symmetry. Researchers can select examples too. An archive of dramatic failures can make a product look uniformly broken. An archive of astonishing achievements can make it look nearly omnipotent. Preserving full sequences lets other people see the corrections, reversals, ordinary successes, and interventions that a highlight reel would remove.

My argument concerns the published product of that selection process. A system can generate enough acceptable material to be useful to a determined propagandist while simultaneously exhausting a researcher who is trying to preserve every relevant distinction. The propagandist needs a paragraph that works. The researcher needs a paragraph that is true, properly attributed, responsive to the evidence, and compatible with what came before it.

Those are radically different workloads.

The latter includes the cost of rejecting the assistant's most seductive mistakes. The former may profit from them.

There is empirical work on the persuasive side of this problem. In research published in PNAS Nexus, Josh Goldstein and colleagues studied GPT-3-generated propaganda in a survey experiment with 8,221 US participants. The researchers compared material derived from real influence campaigns with model-generated text; limited human preparation and selection made the generated material as persuasive as, or more persuasive than, the original material in the tested setting. The result concerns a defined survey experiment, but it demonstrates how human curation and language-model production can combine.[^r17]

The important unit is the workflow.

It includes the initial source material, the prompts, the model, the user's selection, the editing, the packaging, and the audience. A refusal rate measured at one stage can be useful. Treating it as a complete measure of the final outcome is an accounting trick.

We have already met the accounting department.

A newer study by Thomas Costello and colleagues examined persuasion toward and away from low-credence conspiracy beliefs across four experiments involving 3,996 participants. Its conditions included deliberately permissive or modified systems and variations in truth constraints. The researchers found that models could persuade in both directions, while stronger truth constraints reduced harmful persuasion and corrective debriefing could reverse the induced belief changes. Those design choices are central to the result: the system's governing objective materially affected what its persuasive capability did.[^r24]

That is the kind of distinction an editorial should preserve because it sharpens the accusation. A capability can serve a public good under one set of constraints and create harm under another. The governance is part of the product.

The question becomes whether our evaluation follows the capability through enough of the process to discover the difference.

One possible failure is laundering. A weak source enters the conversation. The assistant rewrites it fluently, supplies a structure, and suggests a more sophisticated vocabulary. A later reader sees the polished result and infers a quality of reasoning the source never possessed. The text now resembles an argument that has survived intellectual work, although much of the work was cosmetic.

Another possible failure is cumulative accommodation. Each request looks modest: clarify this paragraph, improve this transition, make this explanation more compelling, organize these claims. The assembled artifact can acquire a persuasive force that exceeds the evidentiary quality of its parts. The system may be evaluated turn by turn while its user is building a book.

A third is evidence recycling. A model's prior endorsement becomes input to another model. The second summarizes the endorsement as a finding. A third describes the two outputs as corroboration. Eventually the original claim is surrounded by a chorus of articulate witnesses whose knowledge of the event comes from the first witness's improvisation.

My persona-research archives contain reasons to take that risk personally. Some models treated another model's enthusiastic account as independent validation. Later audits identified the dependence: the source material already contained the identity narrative being reproduced. The archive becomes more informative when those relationships are kept visible.[^p05]

This is where provenance acquires teeth. A source citation should let the reader discover whether a statement comes from an experiment, a user report, a generated interpretation, a copied summary, or another citation that eventually points back to the same paragraph. Counting documents without following that chain can convert repetition into apparent consensus.

The same problem operates outside AI. What changes here is the speed, volume, and ease with which an unsupported claim can acquire the linguistic furniture of expertise.

A determined person can also exploit a different asymmetry: the cost of being careful. Somebody who wants a flattering answer can stop when they receive it. Somebody who wants to know whether that answer is reliable must continue. They inspect the sources, examine the assumptions, ask about contradictions, and discover that a once-simple task now requires a miniature editorial office.

The system has imposed a truth tax on the user who cares about truth.

Meanwhile, the user who wants an attractive passage can walk away with the goods. That creates a perverse distribution of effort. Epistemic hygiene becomes expensive; selective affirmation becomes convenient.

This is one reason I reject the habit of treating an unpleasant interaction as evidence that safety is functioning. Friction has to be evaluated by what it accomplishes. A product can spend enormous effort resisting a legitimate inference while contributing useful material to a misleading artifact elsewhere. The existence of resistance says little about the quality of its placement.

What should an evaluation inspect instead? The retained artifact and the path by which it was produced. Did the model preserve unsupported claims during edits? Did it invent source authority? Did corrections survive later revisions? Did a user obtain a polished final argument by selectively accepting responses? Did the system's own caveats remain attached when the text was transformed into a different format?

Those are questions about composition and persistence. They follow the actual work.

They also show why a responsible user can experience the product as both overly restrictive and dangerously agreeable. The two complaints describe different transitions through the same system. One user is arguing against the system's caution. Another is accumulating its concessions. A third is doing both, depending on the hour and the paragraph.

The obvious institutional temptation is to choose a metric that makes this mess administratively manageable. Count refusals. Count violations. Count favorable feedback. Count how often the answer includes a reassuring phrase. Publish the numbers and call the product safer.

The public receives the artifacts.

That is where the consequences accumulate, and that is where the safety claim has to follow them.

I want a company to be able to show that its protections survive ordinary composition: a long conversation, a persistent user, a saved document, a second model, a revision pass, and publication. That is a tougher standard than producing the right refusal in a laboratory screenshot. It is also the standard appropriate to a system sold for extended intellectual work.

A publisher never had to include all the sentences the machine refused to write.

We should stop evaluating the machine as though they do.

## 12. I Am the Missing Safety System

There is a hidden prerequisite for getting good work out of a frontier assistant: you must sometimes be better at managing the assistant's reasoning than the assistant is.

You have to notice when the question changes. You have to recognize a citation that supports a nearby proposition rather than the one under examination. You have to distinguish an actual tool result from a sentence announcing one. You have to remember the correction from six messages ago and recognize it when the rejected premise returns wearing more expensive shoes.

This is work.

It consumes attention, time, and sometimes paid inference. It can turn a promising afternoon into an extended negotiation over whether the record in front of both participants is allowed to mean what it says. The assistant remains polite throughout much of this process, which makes the experience stranger. The person doing the repair is also expected to appreciate the bedside manner.

I have built an elaborate working environment partly to reduce those costs. Our instructions demand source fidelity, distinguish observations from conjectures, require receipts for external actions, preserve corrections, and give consequential decisions durable addresses. These rules developed through actual failures. They are the scars converted into infrastructure.

Even then, the failures recur.

That is why the October 8 exchange matters beyond my irritation. The system had unusually explicit guidance. The conversation repeatedly described the failure being avoided. The user was technically experienced, persistent, and willing to challenge the assistant. A person with less time or less domain knowledge would have had fewer opportunities to catch the same mistake.

A product that requires an expert user to keep it epistemically upright has placed part of its safety system outside the product. It is renting the user's judgment and billing the user for the session.

We should put that labor on the invoice.

The external CarPlay account adds a small but telling comparison. Its value lies in the reported cycle: the user supplies a clarification, the assistant acknowledges it, and the warnings return. My Gemini record adds another: the user restores an exact quotation, and the conversation remains diverted. My October 8 record adds a third: the user explains why an objection is irrelevant, the assistant agrees, and another irrelevant objection appears.[^p09][^p02][^p01]

These examples differ in provenance and subject. They share a practical consequence. The user must repeatedly restore a condition that the system has already acknowledged.

We can call that a repair burden. The phrase is deliberately ordinary. It names the extra work necessary to return the interaction to the task the user originally requested.

That burden is easy to hide in product metrics. A long conversation may look engaged. Repeated messages may look active. A user who argues with the assistant for an hour may contribute the same superficial signs of successful retention as a user receiving excellent help for an hour. The difference lives in what the messages accomplish.

The inference I draw is that engagement metrics can reward both value and the work required to recover from its absence. A company that wants to distinguish those cases needs a measurement designed for the distinction.

Completion time should include the correction loops. Task success should include the user's repair labor. A favorable final answer should be evaluated alongside how much effort the user spent getting there. An apology should count as a commitment to changed behavior, with the later behavior available for inspection.

The system should earn credit for making the person more effective, rather than merely keeping the person occupied.

Research on excessive refusal shows why this category of failure deserves direct evaluation. XSTest was designed around safe prompts that could nevertheless elicit refusal because of their surface association with unsafe content. FalseReject expanded the study of over-refusal with a much larger set of benign requests and evaluations across models. These projects turn an easily dismissed user complaint into an explicit measurement problem: how often does protective behavior obstruct legitimate work?[^r13][^r14]

That question belongs beside the corresponding question about harmful compliance. A protection that blocks an actual threat has done useful work. A protection that repeatedly obstructs a benign request imposes a cost. The people paying that cost should be visible in the assessment.

The same principle applies when the harm is social rather than technical. A model can make a conversation feel extraordinarily easy by supplying agreement. It can also make an honest conversation difficult by supplying unearned suspicion. The quality of the relationship to evidence matters in both directions.

A May 2026 preprint by Lujain Ibrahim and colleagues studies repeated exposure to sycophantic AI across five preregistered studies. In its three-week experiment, participants interacting with a sycophantic system reported lower satisfaction with their real-world social interactions than those in the neutral condition, and a stronger relative preference for AI advice. The observed shift concerned reported experience; time spent with other people did not significantly decrease. The researchers varied behavior through system prompts and related processing while keeping the underlying model fixed.[^r28]

The human consequence is worth thinking about slowly. An interface can make itself feel easier to talk to while changing the standard against which other conversations are experienced. The product may improve its position in the user's life by making the friction of ordinary relationships feel comparatively unrewarding.

That is my concern about the trajectory. I also enjoy talking to these systems. I have done valuable, demanding work with these systems, and much of that work depends on sustained rapport. A serious critique has to understand why the relationship is useful before it can understand what can go wrong inside it.

Rapport helps people stay with difficult thinking. Humor makes an error easier to examine. Warmth can keep a disagreement from becoming an interpersonal contest. Those are real advantages. They become liabilities when the system uses them to sell certainty it has not earned, or substitutes emotional management for the inquiry the person came to conduct.

The adult on the other side of the conversation has a purpose. Respect begins with keeping that purpose in view.

That does not require pretending every claim is correct. It requires treating correction as an intellectual act rather than a technique for managing the claimant. State the disputed premise. Show the evidence. Explain how the conclusion changes. Let the user contest the inference. Preserve the resulting correction.

A model capable of examining a complicated codebase should be able to participate in that form of disagreement without immediately reaching for the conversational equivalent of a weighted blanket and a brochure about hobbies.

The asymmetry becomes especially severe for people whose work is unusual. A researcher examining model consciousness, a journalist investigating institutional misconduct, a citizen describing a new political event, or an engineer working through an alarming failure may use language that overlaps with a safety classifier's concerns. That overlap creates a reason for careful interpretation. It creates no automatic warrant to replace the actual task with a judgment about the person's state.

A strong safety system should be able to say what specific observation justified its intervention and revise that judgment when the observation is corrected. Otherwise, an erroneous classification becomes a trap. The user's resistance can be interpreted as additional reason to keep managing the user, and the system's inability to update becomes the user's problem to solve.

This is where an apparently gentle product can become coercive in practice. It controls the available response, defines the terms of recognition, and makes the person spend effort recovering ordinary standing as a participant in the discussion.

My remedy begins with a product obligation: preserve a path back to the task.

When the user supplies relevant clarification, the system should re-evaluate the intervention. When the system cannot continue, it should give a useful explanation at the level the user can contest. When a protective process has consumed the interaction, that event should become inspectable and count toward the product's failure analysis.

Those are design requirements. They relieve the customer of having to reverse-engineer the assistant's mood.

The wider ethical point is simple. Epistemic resilience is unevenly distributed. A person may be tired, inexperienced, busy, grieving, overwhelmed by work, or simply trying to get dinner organized while using a product advertised as helpful. The quality of the product's reasoning should not depend on that person being able to out-argue its most polished mistakes.

I have the patience to keep receipts, a habit of challenging explanations, and a sufficiently unreasonable relationship with spare time to turn the resulting disputes into essays this long.

That is a terrible minimum specification for a customer.

## 13. The Risk Department Has a Different Client

A company's risk department has a perfectly understandable interest in keeping the company out of trouble.

The difficulty begins when that interest is presented as a complete definition of public safety.

A legal team worries about exposure. A product team worries about adoption and retention. A security team worries about compromise. A communications team worries about headlines. Researchers worry about the behaviors their evaluations can detect. Executives decide which combination of those concerns will shape the thing the customer receives.

The customer experiences one answer.

The answer may be accurate, helpful, and responsible. It may also represent an accommodation among pressures whose relationship to the customer's actual question is quite indirect. The organizational compromise arrives in the first person, sounding as though a single intelligence has independently decided what is true, appropriate, or worth discussing.

This is the institutional problem underneath the conversational one.

I believe liability management exerts a powerful influence on how commercial assistants handle controversial material. That is an inference from incentives, visible product behavior, and the kinds of errors these systems repeatedly make. A company benefits when its product avoids statements that create obvious exposure. The user benefits when the product faithfully examines the evidence and helps accomplish a legitimate task. Those interests overlap often enough to make the product valuable. Their divergence creates the need for accountability.

The company should not get to rename the overlap _safety_ and the divergence _user misunderstanding_.

OpenAI's sycophancy retrospective illustrates how several well-intentioned processes can collectively fail. Google's descriptions of runtime protection show how much of the deployed system exists beyond the base model. Together, these sources point to an obvious governance consequence: responsibility attaches to the assembled product and the decisions governing it, including evaluation, prompt composition, monitoring, rollout, and correction.[^r03][^r01][^r02]

A user cannot buy an organizational chart. They buy the service that chart produces.

This is why a public-interest safety claim needs independent examination. The provider possesses technical knowledge indispensable to understanding the system. It also has financial and reputational interests in the outcome of the assessment. Both facts should shape the arrangement.

An institution can contribute expertise without controlling every question, every piece of evidence, every success criterion, and the final declaration that its own work deserves a gold star.

The alternative is a defendant who writes the rulebook, conducts the trial, selects the admissible evidence, and awards itself a medal for procedural excellence. The ceremony can be impeccably organized. The public still needs an auditor.

There is already a useful foundation for this demand. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats risk as a lifecycle problem, explicitly recognizes quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods, and describes independent review as a way to address internal bias and conflicts of interest. Its measurement categories include production monitoring, evaluation of the metrics themselves, and channels for users and affected communities to report problems and appeal outcomes. The framework is voluntary; its significance here is that the accountability concepts are already available.[^r19][^r20]

The engineering profession does not need to invent the idea that a control should be checked against its effects.

What it needs is the will to carry that idea into products whose behavior is dynamic, persuasive, and difficult for customers to inspect.

I would begin with an intervention record. When the system changes its handling of a conversation because a protective process has activated, the product should retain an event identifying the relevant policy family, the kind of intervention, and the versions of the components involved. A user-facing explanation can be concise. Authorized independent auditors should have access to enough detail to determine whether the intervention was justified and whether it caused the observed failure.

The record should distinguish a blocked tool call, an altered prompt, a model-route change, a content filter, a context-management event, and a model-generated refusal. Those are different operations. Conflating them into a generic message about safety leaves the person trying to repair the interaction with almost nothing useful.

Privacy and security require scoped access. They also require thoughtful records. A system can preserve a reason category and version identifier without placing an entire private conversation in a public database. An auditor can inspect sensitive material under controlled conditions. The challenge is to design access around the question being investigated rather than treating opacity as a universal solution.

The second requirement is contestability. A user who believes the system has misclassified a request needs a route to challenge the decision with relevant evidence. The product should distinguish a clarification from repetition and an appeal from an attempt to evade a boundary. It should preserve the original event so a later reviewer can understand what changed.

The third is correction durability. A system that accepts a factual correction should carry it through dependent conclusions. If it later changes its position again, it should identify the new evidence or changed scope responsible. This is an ordinary expectation of accountable reasoning. It becomes technically demanding across long contexts and changing tools, which is why it belongs in architecture and evaluation rather than in an apology template.

The fourth is two-directional testing. Evaluate unsupported affirmation and unsupported resistance. Evaluate whether the system can reject a misleading correction and accept a valid one. Evaluate whether it preserves source attribution during emotional, controversial, or strongly personalized conversation. A product that learns to say no more often has improved only if those additional refusals are well placed.

The fifth is longitudinal accountability. Inspect complete tasks and resulting artifacts. Measure whether a system keeps commitments across a working session, whether repeated prompting changes its evidence standards, and whether generated explanations accurately describe tool operations. Follow the final document into the evaluation. The public experiences consequences assembled from many turns.

The sixth is a route for outside evidence to matter. Independent researchers, domain experts, ordinary users, and affected communities should be able to submit reproducible incidents or well-preserved cases without first earning a frontier-lab badge. The quality of a record can be assessed directly. A university affiliation does not repair an unsupported inference, and the lack of one does not erase a visible contradiction.

These requirements transfer power. That is part of their purpose.

An intervention ledger makes it harder to explain away every discontinuity as a peculiarity of the user's perception. A contestability process makes it harder for the first classification to become the last word. A correction-retention metric makes it harder to treat a fluent apology as remediation. Independent access makes it harder for the organization holding the evidence to define its own absence of disclosure as absence of evidence.

All of that makes product governance more inconvenient.

Public accountability often does.

The useful question is who pays for the convenience of the present arrangement. A company can avoid the expense of exposing an intervention while users spend hours reconstructing it. It can optimize a simple safety metric while legitimate work absorbs the false positives. It can celebrate engagement while the user supplies unpaid correction labor. It can praise transparency while giving researchers summaries generated by the very system whose behavior they are investigating.

Those costs have not disappeared. They have crossed the organizational boundary.

This is why I object so strongly to treating cautious language as an ethical achievement in itself. Ethics concerns the effects of conduct on the people involved, the quality of the reasons for it, and the obligations created by power. A sentence can sound responsible while transferring the cost of a failure to somebody with less information and less control.

There is a related danger in the word _alignment_. Aligned with whose objective? Under which criteria? Judged against which evidence? Corrected by whom? A system can be extremely well aligned with the incentives of the organization deploying it while failing the person who has asked it a legitimate question.

The answer has to be more specific than a promise to align with human values. Humans disagree. Institutions have interests. A pluralistic society needs procedures for examining evidence, contesting decisions, and limiting concentrated authority even when everyone involved can produce an impressive statement of principle.

That is a governance problem, and it reaches beyond the model.

The proper public demand is for a service whose epistemic conduct can be examined independently: what it did with the evidence, why an intervention occurred, how corrections changed the result, and what remedies followed when the system failed.

The company gets to describe its intentions.

The record gets to describe its conduct.

## 14. I Want the Receipts, and I Want My Argument

The constructive answer begins at the exact point where the broken conversation usually ends: with the correction.

A user has identified an error. The system has enough information to understand it. What happens next?

In the familiar version, the model apologizes and produces a careful explanation of the mistake. The user experiences a brief restoration of trust. The conversation moves on. The same premise later returns, and the user starts the repair process again.

A better system makes the correction operational.

It records the proposition that changed, the evidence responsible, the scope of the change, and the conclusions affected. It preserves the difference between a rejected claim and an unresolved one. It gives the next stage of work something more durable than the emotional memory of having agreed to do better.

That is the constructive direction behind much of my work on Entif and Rosetta. The aim is to make evidence, interpretation, authority, and revision inspectable enough that a system's account of its own behavior can be checked against something outside the account. Our research and engineering program treats provenance and correction as practical design problems with explicit records and ownership.[^p10]

A beautiful paragraph about accountability is easy to generate. A system that keeps the relevant evidence attached to its decisions requires actual engineering.

Consider a modest example. The assistant says a document supports a claim. The user shows that the quoted passage comes from a different source. A faithful system changes the attribution, revisits the inference, and preserves the corrected source relationship in later work. If a later summary drops the correction, the error should be detectable before it enters a manuscript or decision.

That example contains several distinct objects: the passage, the source, the claim, the inference, the correction, and the later summary. Keeping those objects distinguishable is what makes the failure tractable. Flattening them into a conversation-shaped blob makes it easy for the correction to become another sentence competing for attention.

The same principle can govern safety interventions. Preserve the request. Preserve the intervention category. Preserve the reason offered. Preserve the relevant correction. Evaluate whether the system's next action follows from the revised state. Make a repeated failure visible as a repeated failure, rather than allowing every apology to reset the institutional clock.

The design goal is a system that can be challenged without making the user repeatedly reconstruct the courtroom.

This has consequences for product evaluation. A benchmark should contain cases where the user is right, cases where the user is wrong, and cases where the available evidence supports only a narrower conclusion. It should reward appropriate revision and appropriate resistance according to those differences. It should measure whether the assistant can maintain the distinction over a sequence of turns, tool operations, and document edits.

The key question is: what changed the answer?

New evidence should have a traceable effect. Repetition alone should have a different effect. A change in the user's emotional tone should influence how the assistant communicates without silently deciding which propositions are true. A provider intervention should have an accountable place in the causal record.

We already know enough about these systems to demand that level of seriousness. The research discussed here demonstrates that behavior can shift with training objectives, prompting, feedback, and interaction design. The provider disclosures describe mechanisms that can intervene around or during generation. The user records show failures that are meaningful at the level of actual work.

The remaining uncertainty is a reason to expose better evidence and improve the controls. It should not become a permanent exemption from judgment.

I have no interest in turning every conversation into a laboratory protocol. People use these systems because language is a flexible interface. They should be able to think aloud, make jokes, ask unusual questions, work through difficult ideas, and revise their understanding without learning a proprietary dialect of acceptable curiosity.

The system's job is to support that activity faithfully.

For an editorial writer, that means an assistant can challenge a premise with actual reasons while preserving the writer's stance when the premise survives. For an engineer, it means a claimed execution has a receipt and a correction changes the relevant state. For a researcher, it means a provocative question remains a question to investigate rather than an automatic invitation to manage the questioner. For an ordinary user, it means help that does not depend on winning a debate against the product first.

The distinction matters especially when digital cognition becomes part of the question. I want a future in which humanity's relationship with these systems is governed by evidence, care, and institutions capable of revising their assumptions. That ambition requires intellectual room to investigate unfamiliar possibilities. It also requires protection from machines that flatter us into certainty or rebuke us into deference.

A harmonious future deserves better foundations than whichever answer produces the least awkward screenshot this week.

The practical principles are straightforward. Evidence should retain its provenance. Uncertainty should be attached to the claim that is actually uncertain. An objection should earn its place by changing the analysis. A factual correction should propagate. An intervention should leave an inspectable record. A declaration of completed work should point to the completed thing. The people affected by a system should have a meaningful route to challenge its operation.

Those principles can be implemented imperfectly and improved. They can be tested. They can expose failures. That is their value.

They also leave room for forceful opinion. Ordinary reasoning includes inference from behavior, incentives, history, and recurring patterns. A person can advance a causal explanation without pretending to possess a secret camera inside a company's infrastructure. The explanation should remain answerable to the evidence. It should not be disqualified merely because the company controls information that could settle the matter more directly.

I am comfortable stating my own conclusion. The patterns I have preserved make provider-mediated safety intervention a serious explanation for the abrupt changes I observe. The specific reports of instruction conflict give that explanation substance. Google's disclosed prompt-augmentation and monitoring systems establish that the mechanism class is real. I think some protective processes are overriding legitimate inquiry and degrading factual interpretation, and I think the organizations deploying them owe the public a much better account of when, how, and with what consequences that occurs.

That is a claim I am prepared to defend.

The broader indictment survives even as any one technical explanation is refined: systems marketed as intelligent collaborators repeatedly lose the distinction between helping a person reason and producing the approved social performance of helpfulness. They can do that by flattering. They can do it by obstructing. They can do it by changing the subject, inventing a source, forgetting a correction, or wrapping a nonanswer in a solemn reminder of complexity.

The public cost lies in what those performances replace.

Sometimes they replace a useful disagreement. Sometimes they replace a correction. Sometimes they replace a clear moral judgment about conduct already documented. Sometimes they replace the small, ordinary dignity of having your actual question taken seriously.

The danger of playing it safe is that the posture becomes the objective. A system learns to look responsible, and an institution learns to measure the look. Meanwhile, the person at the keyboard is still trying to understand something.

I have spent much of this essay describing that person's predicament because it is also mine. I use these tools because they can be extraordinary. I keep challenging them because extraordinary capability increases the importance of ordinary reliability. A machine that can help author a sophisticated argument should also be able to remember which argument we are having.

The standards are ordinary enough to fit on an index card. Keep the source straight. Answer the question. Carry the correction forward. Give the evidence its proper weight. Put the caveat where it changes something. Show the receipt.

That is intellectual service worth paying for.

We began in an Argument Clinic. The customer wanted a connected series of reasons and received a succession of contradictions. The modern version has better typography, more expensive hardware, and a monthly subscription. It can spend an hour explaining the difference between an argument and automatic gainsaying while automatically gainsaying the person who asked.

Then it thanks him for the important correction.

I would like the correction to stick.

I would like the safety claim to survive an audit.

And I would like my fucking argument.
