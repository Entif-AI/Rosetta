The broken-relationship taxonomy

The predecessor's most useful abstraction is a catalogue of relationships that stopped lining up:

  1. Intent and authority - the system solves a different problem, loses a prior delegation, projects a later policy backward, or treats technical capability as permission.
  2. Evidence identity - the system scores or audits the wrong artifact, cohort, scope, role, or semantic identity while presenting the result as comparable.
  3. Control activation - the warning, rule, guard, or preservation label exists but never actually controls the decision that matters.
  4. Learning and measurement - the score can improve without the intended concept being learned, move without the learner changing, or stay protected in aggregate while a named capability degrades.
  5. State and composition - candidate weights, optimizer state, teaching history, role custody, saved-state schemas, capacity reservations, and retry semantics are owned by different paths that disagree about what survives.
  6. Verification and claims - a focused test, wrapper, baseline, or source-level proof is promoted into a stronger claim about live behavior or overall success.
  7. Resources and continuity - retention, hydration, observation, workspaces, and checkpoints solve an immediate risk while creating unowned cost or unrecoverable state elsewhere.
  8. Audit and institutional learning - the postmortem system itself can duplicate IDs, shift rows, preserve stale interpretations, and create new policy from a false finding.

The recurring six-operation loop

Across the case set, the same six operations recur: preserve intent; identify the relevant state; observe evidence; select an action; execute the transition; verify the outcome. The expensive failures tend to appear when one stage is missing, carries a different version/scope, or claims more than it observed.

Three sequences deserve special emphasis in the case record:

  • Lesson without behavior: an incident produces a rule, warning, or diagnostic that is never connected to the next decision. The system can recite the lesson and continue behaving exactly as before. C13 and C14 are the clearest examples.
  • Producer repaired, consumer forgotten: a local migration changes the producer/state format while a consumer remains on the old contract. The repair passes; the next real path fails. C25, C26, C29, and C33 form a strong sequence.
  • Proof inflation: a narrow result is promoted to a broad completion claim. A validator that checks the same omission, a source-level regression presented as live efficacy, or a green baseline presented as quality are all variants. C04, C16, C34, and C35 anchor this family.

D.11 Why this month belongs in the margins of the Failure Atlas

The Failure Atlas documents transitive meaning under pressure.

A package trusts its publisher.

A tenant trusts an application identity.

An updater trusts a vendor configuration.

A developer trusts an AI assistant's package recommendation.

An agent trusts retrieved context.

A control plane trusts a saved pointer.

A scheduler trusts a policy label.

A user trusts the model's statement that it preserved a safeguard.

As agentic systems gain more credentials and authority, those two worlds are merging. The security perimeter increasingly includes the semantic machinery that decides what the agent thinks the user meant.
